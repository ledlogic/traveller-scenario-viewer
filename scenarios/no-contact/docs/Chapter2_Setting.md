## 2.1: THE WORLD, JORJOR

**Jorjor** *(pronounced "hor-HOR", J as a breathy Spanish H, equal stress on both syllables; yes, it's definitely a hor-hor adventure)*, Arcturus Subsector, Solomani Rim Sector, hex 3225. UWP **B641888-7**.

The scenario takes place on **day 245 of Y1116** (Imperial date 245-1116). The world and regional history timeline is in §2.20.

![Subsector map: Jorjor (hex 3225) and its neighbours](img/jorjor-subsector-map.webp)

| UWP element | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Starport | B | Good quality: refined fuel, shipyard, both highport and downport. Real, unremarkable ship traffic. |
| Size | 6 | Medium, 9,600 km diameter |
| Atmosphere | 4 | Thin, tainted |
| Hydrographics | 1 | Dry world, 10% surface water |
| Population | 8 | Moderate, ~100 million |
| Government | 8 | Civil Service Bureaucracy |
| Law | 8 | High Law: all blades, stunners and visible armour banned, and every lower-level ban also applies (firearms, shotguns, concealable weapons, military and energy weapons, explosives, poisons). Police, rangers and permit holders are exempt (§2.32) |
| Tech Level | 7 | Pre-stellar electronics |

Jorjor is a **hellworld**, poor for its size, never became the industrial power it hoped to be, and its people are described as grim realists who survive daily hardship. That's exactly why it's a good place to disappear. The Class B starport means real ship traffic in and out, so a late extraction reads as *late*, not *suspicious*, at first.

Jorjor sits in the same subsector (Arcturus) that *Signal GK* uses for Baldwin Wei's home turf, keeping this scenario in continuity with existing SolSec/Wei material.

## 2.2: THE TOWN AND ITS SURROUNDINGS

The motel sits in a near-ghost settlement on the old alignment of the highway across Jorjor's flats, roughly halfway between two larger places that no longer need it. It is isolated, sun-blasted and once busier than it is now, kept alive almost entirely by one roadside business.

Key details of the town layout:

- **One business carries the whole town.** Motel Yermo is not *a* business in town, it is functionally the reason the settlement exists: gas, food and lodging under one near-24-hour operation, with a tall neon sign visible for miles across flat desert. At its peak, in the years when the highway mattered, it employed a meaningful fraction of the town's own population.
- **A bypass gutted it.** The town thrived on through-traffic until a newer highway alignment opened on a route that did not connect to this stretch of road at all, and business went to almost zero overnight. This fits Jorjor's "hellworld, never became the industrial power it hoped to be" characterization (Chapter 2, §2.1): a regional bypass (a newer highway alignment, a rerouted rail spur, a shifted starport shuttle corridor) quietly stranded this stretch of road years ago, which explains why it is this quiet and this easy to disappear into. **Resolved:** the bypass runs the opposite direction from the Strange Park (§2.16); the old road, the one the motel sits on, is the one that leads toward the Reserve; the new route leads away from it entirely. Officially the realignment was pure economics, a shorter or more efficient route for regional traffic. Unofficially, it also happened to route most through-traffic away from the one stretch of road where bad interactions with the Reserve or its truck route were most likely; a convenient side effect the bureaucracy never needed to name or justify, since the paperwork only had to say "efficiency" to be procedurally complete. Whether anyone actually engineered it that way on purpose, or it was simply a fortunate accident the Civil Service Bureaucracy quietly noticed and never corrected, is left to the referee, either answer fits the government type's established texture (Chapter 4, §4.7) equally well.
- **A population that shrank to almost nothing.** The settlement ran from several hundred people at its peak down to single digits, and the motel is kept alive as much by stubbornness as by real commerce. It should read as a real place with real history, not a purpose-built stage set, sustained by just enough passing traffic to stay open.
- **Distances to services.** The nearest services are far away: a **15 km** filling station, a **60 km** town garage and a **90 km** industrial site, the same figures used in the vehicle-repair table in Chapter 5, §5.3. "Just drive to the next town" is a real ordeal, not an inconvenience.
- **A fenced site just outside town.** A few kilometres out, a stretch of scrub is fenced off as a "geological survey" site. This is the seed for the **Fence-Line Hum** (Chapter 2, §2.8), and it gives the table a concrete visual anchor for that rumor rather than leaving it purely verbal.

## 2.4: WHY THIS MATTERS AT THE TABLE

A faded roadside town carries a specific emotional register (faded rather than sinister, populated by people who stayed out of stubbornness or had nowhere better to go) that suits this scenario's tone better than a generic "spooky desert motel" would. Leaning on that texture (one business holding a whole town together, a highway that used to matter more than it does now, distances that make "just drive to the next town" a real ordeal) gives every other chapter's atmospheric and logistical details a grounded reason to be true, rather than existing only because the plot needs them to.

## 2.5: WHY THEY FEEL TRAPPED

All six agents took **one-way rides** to reach this motel (a courier drop-off, a hired local driver, a hitched ride that continued on without them) on the assumption that extraction would be their ride *out*. None of them arranged their own return transport, because none of them expected to need it. That's the trap: it's not a locked door, it's the quiet, sinking realization over the course of the night that nobody has a car, nobody knows a reliable local contact, and the nearest town is a long walk down a two-lane highway in the dark.

Layer in two concrete, external complications:

- **A dust storm (a haboob, a wall of dust about a kilometre high, pushed ahead of a storm front) is blocking regional traffic** on the highway approach (see Chapter 2, §2.31 for its clock). It is real and checkable (a radio weather bulletin, a trucker who mentions it, downed branches visible from the parking lot), not a mystical omen. It's the reason a normal ride out isn't simple even before anyone suspects anything's wrong.

  *Read-aloud:* "A wall of dust a kilometre high, walking toward you across the flats. You can hear it before you can see it, a rising hiss like a hundred hands rubbing sandpaper."
- **Local commercial comms are out**, knocked down by the same storm, payphones dead, the motel office's own line down. This is what makes the missed extraction ambiguous rather than immediately alarming: is there no contact because the extraction failed, or just because the storm took the lines down? Neither the players nor their characters can tell early on, and that ambiguity should be played straight, not tipped either way by the referee's tone.

## 2.6: TECH LEVEL DISCIPLINE, WHAT GETS NOTICED

Yes, **using gear above the local TL7 baseline risks detection**, and this should be a standing pressure the whole session, not a one-time rule. Jorjor's starport and regional infrastructure include TL7-appropriate detection: passive radio-spectrum monitoring (routine, automated, watching for exactly the kind of anomalous signal a TL10+ transmitter or scanner throws off), and, because Jorjor sits in Imperial space, any signal that *is* flagged gets escalated to the **Imperial Security Service**, not just local police. A flagged signal doesn't mean immediate arrest; it means someone starts asking the local bureaucracy pointed questions, which is its own slow-building threat given Chapter 4, §4.7.

**House rule for the table:** any use of TL10+ gear for anything beyond brief, low-power, one-off activity (a quick burst transmission, a single scan) should call for a **Electronics (comms) or Stealth check** against a DM set by how sustained or high-power the use is. A failed check doesn't mean instant Imperial agents at the door; it means a detection event has occurred, banked quietly by the referee, that surfaces later (a "routine" knock from a bureaucratic inspector, a stranger's car that starts idling outside, per Chapter 7, §7.1's complication options) rather than immediately.

**The practical implication for players:** anyone using their real tradecraft gear has to weigh capability against exposure, and the TL12 gear carried by the Imperial spy in the lot (Chapter 4, §4.6) is the loudest thing on the highway tonight. The Mass notices TL10+ emissions (§2.30), and the spy's receiver is what draws the gutwings to the motel first (Chapter 6, §6.4). That gives sharp players something concrete to watch for without the referee needing to hand-wave a "vibes-based" tell.

## 2.7: RANDOM TABLE, TRAFFIC THAT DOESN'T STOP

| 1D8 | Event |
|---|---|

| 5 | Local law enforcement patrol car passes at normal speed, doesn't slow, spotlight briefly sweeps the motel lot out of routine, not suspicion. |

## 2.8: LOCAL FLORA & FAUNA

Jorjor's thin, tainted atmosphere and dry (10% water) surface support sparse, tough native life; nothing native is aggressive toward humans by default, but the environment itself is what makes going outside at night unpleasant rather than dangerous:

- **Scrub-thorn**: low, wiry desert brush that catches clothing and makes quiet movement across open ground genuinely difficult (DM−2 to Stealth checks moving through it off the beaten path).
- **Wind-chitters**: small, nocturnal, insect-analog swarms that produce a constant dry clicking/rattling sound across the open desert at night, unsettling ambient noise, harmless, but excellent cover for masking (or mistaking) the sound of an actual approach.
- **Dust-runners**: fist-sized ground-burrowing scavengers, skittish, harmless, but their sudden scrabbling movement in peripheral vision is a reliable source of false alarms and jump-scares for anyone stepping outside.
- **The Fence-Line Hum** (optional): roughly 4km out, a stretch of scrub-desert fenced off decades ago by the Civil Service Bureaucracy (Chapter 4, §4.7) as an unexplained hazard site, officially logged as "geological survey, access restricted." Locals who'll talk about it at all describe a low, directionless hum audible only at night, wind-chitters going dead silent within the fence line, and a standing rule never to bring metal tools past the posted markers. Nobody's filed a real incident report in years; the fence, like everything else in Jorjor's bureaucracy, mostly persists because nobody's filed the correct form to take it down. Play this strictly as unresolved texture: a rumor a PC might hear in the lobby (§2.9, or §2.12) or from the motel owner, never a site the scenario actually visits. It exists to make Jorjor feel like a world with older, stranger history than tonight's plot touches, not a plot hook to chase.

Nothing on Jorjor should physically threaten the PCs outdoors; the fear is psychological and logistical (visibility, footing, disorientation in unfamiliar scrub-desert dark), not predation.

## 2.10: DAILY LIFE & INSTITUTIONS

Texture for how all of the above actually feels to live inside, day to day:

- **School**: reading only; no testing. Literacy is maintained as a bare procedural formality. Testing would generate records, records would generate audits, and audits are the one thing this town's whole quiet arrangement can't survive.
- **Medicine**: weak salves, creams, and home therapy. No real clinic or pharmacy investment; anything serious means a long trip elsewhere, which circles back to Chapter 5, §5.3's vehicle-repair problem being a genuine ordeal, not an inconvenience.
- **Law**: frontier justice by bureaucratic whim: standard practice for a dispute is to detain *both* parties until the relevant paperwork is fully settled, which, given the Big Queue below, can take weeks. Nobody wants the law involved for anything short of true necessity, which is exactly the outcome the arrangement quietly depends on.
- **Entertainment**: radio dramas, drawn out even further than old real-world serials like *Dark Shadows*; the pacing itself feels like another symptom of everything else here running at half-speed.
- **The Big Queue ("the BQ")**: the single ordering system for essentially every civil action: vehicle repairs, road and bridge fixes, permit approvals, anything requiring the bureaucracy to actually do something. An infamous, sprawling, strictly first-come queue that never seems to move; a running local joke and a running local despair in equal measure, and a reliable, low-cost detail to drop into any NPC's small talk (the motel owner grumbling about a repair still stuck in the BQ, a restaurant regular complaining about a permit filed months ago) without it needing to connect to anything else in this document set.


### The Civil Service Bureaucracy on Jorjor

*Source: the Civil Service Bureaucracy government type in 101 Governments, adapted for this scenario. Jorjor mixes three of its flavours.*

**Who rules.** Since **Y410** the world has been run by agencies staffed by career administrators, not by elected leaders or lords. Law is not made by a legislature, it is issued as **regulations** drafted by departments, and it is applied by whichever department holds the form. Jorjor is Law Level 8 (§2.32), but the law is enforced through paper, not through police, so the real power belongs to whoever controls the stamp.

**Three flavours, one world.**
- **The Lamp in the Dark: forms, stamps and style.** Recruits come from the best the schools produce and are trained in the Bureau's ethic, starting in the lowly departments and rising by patience and talent. Every service needs the correct form, stamped by the correct office, and filed in a pending tray. Dissenters are not crushed, they are offered a clerkship. This is why the people who know the Reserve is wrong are inside the Bureaucracy and still file the paperwork.
- **Jobsworth's World: nobody does what they are not paid to do.** Duties and demarcation lines are exact and jealously guarded. A ranger will not leave a post, and a rescue team will not leave the gate, because the rule says "stand down" and nothing in the rule says why. Counters close for the shift change and the relief shift is always three minutes late.
- **Non-Profit Reward: departments that compete to look important.** Offices, staff and budgets matter more than results, and the system survives through quiet collusion with the people who profit from it, here the shippers, the contractors and the Reserve's own backers (§2.24). The common people subsidise their own exploiters.

**How it plays at the table.** Treat these as house rules for any dealing with an official or a form tonight.
- **Delay.** Any official request takes **1D x 10 minutes** (1D hours for a major one) while the clerks sort out whose job it is. A traveller who persuades a clerk to use the "urgent" tray (Persuade or Admin, Average, 8+) halves the delay and no more, because the system has little flexibility.
- **Fixed prices.** Prices for most goods are set by regulation, not by supply, so some things are far too cheap and some far too dear. A good Broker or Streetwise roll (Average, 8+) gets a deal about 5% better. A poor one costs about 5% more.
- **The stamp beats the truth.** A properly stamped form is believed over a true account. A bluff or request with the right stamp, seal or reference number gets **DM +2**, and one without a stamp gets **DM -2**. This is why a stamped manifest gets a feed truck past a Red-alert Gate (Chapter 7, §7.3) and why "departed without notice" is believed (Chapter 7, §7.7).
- **Inefficiency is a feature.** Nobody can be persuaded that there is a faster way, but everyone can be persuaded to put a form in a different tray. If the table tries to cut a corner, the answer is "which form?"

**The Bureaucracy and the Reserve.** The Bureaucracy is not secretly evil. It is simply incapable of noticing that Concession 9 should have been reviewed, because the paperwork was complete (§2.18, §2.20). Departments each hold a slice of the Reserve's record, spelled five different ways (Chapter 7, §7.7), and each only has to say "reviewed" to be procedurally correct. The Compliance Officer, the Rescue Service and the Gate each do their job exactly, and the result is that four people vanish without anyone breaking a rule (Chapter 4, §4.11 and §4.12).

**Forms to know.** The scenario's recurring paperwork: **Form 11-C** (Subsidence, Minor), **Form 7-D** (Departed Without Notice), **Form 14-R** (Request to Retrieve Property from a Closed Area), **Form 31-T** (Transfer of Custody), **Form 22-M** (Unclaimed Remains), **Policy RS-9** (the Rescue Service stand-down) and **Schedule Item 9** (Concession 9 itself). Chapter 7, §7.7 explains how each can be used as a weapon.

**Local officials.** **Deputy Comptroller Osei** (Chapter 4, §4.7) works in the Off-World Manifest office, where flawless paperwork has hidden his gambling debt and his lack of technical skill. **Compliance Officer Pruitt** (Chapter 4, §4.11) is the one clerk who has noticed the pattern. **Captain Pell** (Chapter 4, §4.12) keeps an honest record in a system that does not ask for one. **Cabo Marsh** (Chapter 4, §4.5) enforces the law by detaining both parties until the paperwork is settled (the Big Queue above).

## 2.12: THE LOBBY

Small, worn, and functional: a few vinyl-cushioned chairs, a coin-op **vending machine** (soda, cigarettes, stale snacks, TL7, coin-operated, no smart tech to malfunction dramatically), a **jukebox or radio** playing low static-tinged music most of the night, and (inconsistently, depending on the owner's mood) a pot of coffee or a plate of something at the front desk.

It is the **only route** from any guest room to the parking lot and highway (Chapter 2, §2.11), which makes it the natural place for any two guests, PC or civilian, to cross paths without it being suspicious. Use it as a pressure valve: a scene that's gotten too intense in one room can spill into the lobby and suddenly involve someone who has no idea what's actually going on.

**Random lobby event table**, roll or pick 1D6 whenever a PC passes through, or once per shift as scene-setting:

| 1D6 | Event |
|---|---|
| 1 | The jukebox skips on the same few bars of a song for an uncomfortably long stretch before someone fixes it. |
| 2 | The vending machine eats someone's coins. A small, human, deflating moment of frustration, good icebreaker material between strangers. |
| 3 | The motel owner (Chapter 4, §4.1) is at the desk, in a talkative mood, with an opinion about the weather, the storm, or "the usual crowd" this stretch of highway gets. |
| 4 | One of the Room 4 civilian guests (§4.2) is already here, doing something mundane, reading a paper, nursing a coffee, watching the door. |
| 5 | The lobby is briefly, completely empty; an unsettling contrast if the last few scenes have been tense elsewhere. |
| 6 | Someone new comes through the front door, not a guest checking in, just passing through to ask directions or use the payphone (which, per Chapter 2, §2.5, is dead due to the storm). |

## 2.13: THE RESTAURANT: TONE REFERENCE

This space is built to evoke the atmosphere of *Dying Room Only* (1973); a Richard Matheson TV movie in which a couple stops at a remote desert diner, the husband vanishes from an enclosed space (the men's room) without a trace, and the diner's two staff/regulars respond to the wife's search with cold, evasive non-help that reads as either hostility or complicity, never clearly which. No special effects, no monster, just an insular, unhelpful small space that makes outsiders feel unwelcome and increasingly unsafe. This section borrows that register, not the plot; nothing here should resolve as supernatural.

## 2.14: THE RESTAURANT: THE SPACE

Attached to the motel but not the same building as the lobby (Chapter 2, §2.11); a separate, low structure a short walk across the gravel lot, connected by a covered walkway. **Officially semi-closed:** posted hours say lunch and early dinner only, but the lights are sometimes on late, and whether it's "open" on any given night depends entirely on whether the staff feel like it.

Worn vinyl booths, a long counter, a pass-through kitchen window, a jukebox older and less functional than the one in the lobby (Chapter 2, §2.12), and, the one detail worth making concrete for the table, a **single, small restroom hallway** at the back with one entrance and no other way out, just large enough for one person to be out of sight of the dining room for a stretch of time without it being unusual.

## 2.15: OPTIONAL USE, THE VANISHING BEAT

If a referee wants a hard, memorable Graveyard-shift complication (Chapter 7, §7.1) beyond what's already in §2.7–2.9, this space supports one directly: **someone goes to the restroom and doesn't come back.**

- Best used on a low-stakes NPC rather than a PC, **Del or Priya Ashworth** (Chapter 4, §4.2) is the natural choice, since their disappearance genuinely worries the table without derailing anyone's own character arc.
- Play the staff exactly as unhelpful as the tone reference: not obstructive enough to look like a smoking gun, just unhelpful enough that a search feels harder than it should. "Ain't seen anyone come through here tonight" delivered flatly, even if it isn't quite true.
- **Resolve this mundanely.** The point isn't a hidden trapdoor or a kidnapping ring; it's the *feeling* of one for as long as the search takes. Good mundane resolutions: the missing person stepped out the back to get sick from the heat and lost track of time; they fell asleep in a booth in a back room the staff didn't think to mention; they walked back to Room 4 without saying anything, assuming everyone would notice. Let the dread do the work; don't cash it in for anything stranger than an ordinary, slightly embarrassing explanation.
- If a table wants higher stakes than that, the ceiling should still stay mundane and human; a genuine but ordinary crime of opportunity (theft, a scuffle) rather than anything connected to SolSec, Wei, or Osei. Keeping this space's mystery separate from the main plot's is what makes it work as pure atmosphere rather than a second, competing mystery.

## 2.16: THE STRANGE PARK (THE GUT)

Roughly an hour's drive from the motel, off a spur road most maps don't bother labeling, and in the opposite direction from the regional bypass (§2.2) that stranded this stretch of road, sits a fenced, half-abandoned tourist site the locals just call **the Gut**, officially the **Halvorsen Basin Biological Reserve**, discovered by a rancher-turned-prospector (Ansel Halvorsen, **~Y1097**) about two decades before tonight, made a government Reserve in **Y1101**, and struck by a disaster in **Y1113** (the Terrace collapse in Areas 12–14) that closed it to the public until it was **relicensed in Y1114** for escorted tours and camping. What's down there (a colossal, still-living subsurface ecosystem, a thousand years in the making) is entirely original to this document set, not drawn from any existing published setting. **It is no longer background.** §2.17 to §2.26 explain where it came from, §2.27 onward is the Reserve in play, and Chapter 4, §4.8 is the SolSec pickup team that vanished into it: it is the reason the extraction never came.

### Why the biowaste trucks

The Reserve's continued (unpublicized) survival depends on a standing contract, administered, inevitably, through Jorjor's Civil Service Bureaucracy (Chapter 4, §4.7): regional slaughterhouses and agricultural operations, plus sector-based systems (other worlds across the Rim sending their own biological waste), pay a modest fee to have their organic waste hauled to the edge of the flats and dumped, under a permit whose paperwork describes it as routine "organic waste processing." In practice, it's feed; a thousand-year-old, half- forgotten arrangement (**Concession 9**, Chapter 2, §2.18) that keeps the organism sedated and quiet rather than hungry and active. Almost nobody administering the contract today knows *why* it exists; it survives the same way Osei's compromised clearances do (Chapter 4, §4.7); because a bureaucracy this committed to procedure treats a correctly-filed permit as self-justifying, and questioning a thousand-year-old arrangement is itself the irregular act.

**Referee use:** the truck route to the flats is a good source of incidental color for the highway traffic table (Chapter 2, §2.7); a heavy, slow-moving truck at an odd hour, smelling faintly wrong, that nobody local thinks twice about. Played straight, it's just one more piece of the region's mundane, slightly-off texture; a referee running a longer Jorjor campaign can dig much further into it whenever they're ready.

### The ancient satellite and the shutter

High overhead, in a stable orbit nobody local claims to have put it there, sits an **Ancients-era orbital platform**, precursor technology, vastly older than current Imperial or Solomani spaceflight, generally assumed derelict. Local legend (mostly dismissed as superstition, the same register as the Fence-Line Hum, Chapter 2, §2.8) holds that the platform contains a massive optical system, effectively a solar concentrator, behind a **controllable aperture, "the shutter."** In its current, presumably-jammed-open-or-closed state it does nothing. But if someone understood how to command it, opening the shutter at the right orbital alignment could focus enough sunlight onto a target region to produce a genuine, sustained heatwave, not a laser, not a single blast, but enough concentrated solar energy over a wide area to scorch, dry out, or ignite whatever's underneath.

**This is optional deep lore, not a plot device already wired into No Contact.** It's included here as a seed for a referee who wants a bigger, slower-burning campaign thread across multiple sessions: Who built it? Does anyone currently living know how to control it? Could Osei's bureaucracy, the Ancients themselves, SolSec, or the Imperium already be quietly aware of it? None of those questions need answers for tonight's motel scenario to work; they're here so the referee has somewhere to go next.

## 2.17: THE PIT: OVERVIEW

The Strange Park (Chapter 2, §2.16) used to be background lore. Tonight it is the reason the extraction never came: the SolSec pickup team landed early, toured the Reserve, and got into trouble (Chapter 4, §4.8). This section takes the mystery out of the *origin* of the Gut; it is not Ancient, it is not a weapon, it is not a one-of-a-kind miracle. It is **the Imperium's compost heap**, and it was never meant to be anything else, and answers the question the players will ask ("how did this happen?") in a form you can read aloud.

What stays mysterious is what the heap **became**: §2.27 onward and Chapter 4, §4.8 cover that.

**The one sentence to give the table:** *"For a thousand years, the Imperium paid Jorjor a few credits a ton to take the stuff nobody else wanted. The experiment was thus created: what happens when that process continues unabated."*

## 2.18: THE POLICY

Jorjor's original **Thale Charter (Y31)** was granted to a knight of the Imperium, **Sir Edric Thale**, who was given administrative rights to a poor, thin-aired, mostly-empty world in exchange for holding it quietly. Thale's financial problem was simple: nothing here sells. His solution, entered into the Charter's schedule of fees as **Concession 9**, was equally simple.

> *"The Administrator may, for such fee as he shall set, receive upon the Halvorsen flats such organic wastes, spoilage, offal, cullings and nutrient residues as may be consigned to the world by licensed Imperial shippers, and shall bear no liability for the condition thereof."*

That is the entire policy: **"Concession 9."** Everything since is accretion. Four details make it feel real, not sinister:

- **The fee is modest on purpose.** Roughly **Cr10 per dTon**, unchanged for a millennium because adjusting it would require a form nobody has authority to file. At peak volume that is about **Cr12 million a year**, real money for Jorjor, rounding error for the Imperium.
- **The shippers are respectable.** Agricultural worlds with a harvest glut, slaughterhouse and cannery combines, food-synthesis plants, and (from **Y212**) corporate biolabs disposing of cell lines, culls and failed engineered stock. Every one has a clean manifest. None of them is doing anything illegal.
- **The administrators were knights, not villains.** Thale's heirs and the Knight-Administrators who followed (Y31–Y410) were honest, bored gentry. Every one of them signed off on "just one more contract." None of them could have foreseen what a thousand years of mixed biomass, engineered microbes, and cultured tissue would do when left to stew in a warm basin under an unusually sunny sky.
- **The Bureaucracy kept it going by never questioning it.** After **Y410** the Civil Service Bureaucracy took over Jorjor's administration (Chapter 4, §4.7). It inherited Concession 9 as an item on a schedule, and, true to type, never questioned a properly-stamped form.

## 2.19: THE NUMBERS, HOW MUCH?

**Question:** how many metric tonnes have been dumped on Jorjor in 1,085 years of Concession 9, assuming every corporate biolab and agricultural world with a waste problem used it?

**Method:** arrivals per month and average load per arrival, in three eras. One dTon (14 cubic metres) of wet organic slurry is taken at roughly **14 tonnes** (density about 1 tonne per cubic metre).

| Era | Years | Arrivals / month | Avg. load | Tonnes / ship | Tonnes / year | Era total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Trickle** (Charter, local farms and nearby worlds) | Y31–Y300 (269 yrs) | 2 | 400 dTon | 5,600 | 0.13 Mt | **36 Mt** |
| **The Contract Era** (corporate biolabs, ag worlds join) | Y300–Y800 (500 yrs) | 10 | 1,500 dTon | 21,000 | 2.5 Mt | **1,260 Mt** |
| **The Feed Line** (slurry pipeline + rail spur, everyone ships here) | Y800–Y1116 (316 yrs) | 40 | 2,500 dTon | 35,000 | 16.8 Mt | **5,309 Mt** |
| | | | | **Total** | | **≈ 6,600 Mt ≈ 6.6 billion tonnes** |

**Putting 6.6 billion tonnes in context:**

- About **6.6 cubic kilometres** of material; a cube roughly **1.9 km on a side**, or a 165 square km basin floor covered **40 metres** deep.
- About **2,500 Great Pyramids** of Giza, by mass.
- At the peak rate, **46,000 tonnes arrive every day**; a little under **two tonnes every minute, forever**.
- Credits earned at Cr10/dTon: roughly Cr0.1 million a year in the Trickle, Cr1.8M in the Contract Era, and Cr12M at peak. Over all 1,085 years: **about Cr4.7 billion**: the price of a few warships, spread over a millennium.

**Why the motel only sees a trickle of trucks.** Almost none of this comes by road. After **Y800** it comes through a **slurry pipeline** fed from the orbital downport rail spur, ending at the Feed Line Terminal (§2.29, Area 8). Trucks carry only about **1%**, roughly 15 truck-trips a day at 30 tonnes each, mostly for small contractors, local farms, and specialty loads that need hand-handling. A heavy truck at an odd hour is therefore *normal*, not sinister (Chapter 2, §2.7).

**Decomposition does most of the work.** The Mass (§2.21) has been digesting this waste for centuries; the pile is not 6.6 billion tonnes of standing waste. It is, instead, a *standing biomass* that is still growing, still being fed, and, at this point, no longer entirely depending on the feed.

## 2.21: WHAT IT BECAME (AND WHAT IT STILL ISN'T)

The Gut, locals' name for the **Mass**, is not one animal, plant, or fungus. It's a single, sprawling, interconnected **ecosystem** with a colony's behaviour: a vast root-and-tissue network under the basin (the "Mass"), and a surface population of creatures and plants that are organs of it, not residents in it.

- **Source of the unnatural.** A thousand years of engineered stock, culled cell lines, agricultural pathogens, and lab leftovers, left in a warm, sunny sink, produced something with the *functions* of an organism and no clear identity. A xenobiologist would call it a **superorganism**; the locals call it the Gut.
- **Why it's warm.** Decomposition, plus (optionally) heat from the Ancients' satellite and its shutter (Chapter 2, §2.16 and §2.25). The referee's call whether the satellite is warming the basin on purpose.
- **What is genuinely unknown.** Why it responds to psionics (the Mass *notices* minds, §2.23), why its fauna move as a herd, and whether it is waiting for something. None of these are answered in this scenario.

**Nothing is hidden at the level of history.** If a PC with Admin, Streetwise, or Science (biology) asks the right local, they can learn nearly all of §2.18–§2.20 over a morning's conversation. The *mystery* is not "where did it come from." It is "why is it so alert, and why now?"

## 2.23: THE RESERVE TODAY, THE 10%

The Reserve is **a tourist resort with a body count**, and it is run, quietly, as exactly that.

- **Visitors:** roughly **4,000 a year**. About **10%, roughly 400 people, go missing every year**. Locals call it **"gone kissing the Gut,"** grim slang that has become, over decades, the Reserve's only honest description of itself.
- **How the numbers stay quiet.** Every incident has a form:
  - **Form 7-D (Departed Without Notice)**: a visitor who "left of their own accord." No body, no investigation.
  - **Form 11-C (Subsidence, Minor)**: a ground event with "no reported injuries."
  - **Form 14-R (Request to Retrieve Property from a Closed Area)**: the legitimate way to fetch a lost bag. It is also how the Bureau decides a retrieval is not necessary.
- **The cover-up mechanics are mundane.** Rented vehicles are driven away by staff and returned to rental agencies in other towns. Hotel rooms are checked out remotely. Messages are sent from the visitor's own comm.
- **The UBI float.** Death registration and account closure sit in different departments (Chapter 2, §2.24). A missing visitor's accounts are left open; the bank keeps the float. This is why **nobody at the Bureau has a reason to look harder.**
- **The Mass reacts to minds.** Psionic users are noticed. A character using Telepathy near the basin will see the Mass "look back" (Chapter 2, §2.30).

> *Referee note:* "gone kissing" is local gallows humour for "missing." Reserve staff use it to each other, never in front of visitors. Players who ask a local what it means will get the plain answer at once.

## 2.24: WHERE THE MONEY GOES, THE BODY BANK

Optional background: none of this is wired into tonight's scenario, but it answers the natural follow-up question of how a fenced, half-forgotten reserve and a derelict Ancients platform both stay funded on a poor, unremarkable hellworld nobody's watching closely.

**Imperium-wide UBI, bureaucratic style:** somewhere in the Third Imperium's history, a baseline guaranteed-income program was established, administered, Jorjor being Jorjor, exactly the way a Civil Service Bureaucracy would run it: not as a single cash deposit, but as several **locked-purpose accounts** per citizen (Food, Housing, Transit, and a handful of others) each replenished on its own schedule, non-transferable between categories. Less a modern universal basic income than a permanent, comprehensive descendant of a wartime ration-and-stamp system: everyone gets exactly enough for exactly what the account is named for, administered by exactly the kind of procedure-worshipping apparatus Jorjor's government already runs on (Chapter 4, §4.7).

**The body bank:** the dead bound for the Reserve (§2.16) as biomass pass through the town's own processing office on their way there. Properly, a death registration should trigger account closure the same day. In practice, closure requires a *separate* form, filed by a *different* department than the one that handles death registration; a classic silo failure, and one that happens with quiet, grinding regularity given how routine this specific transfer has become to the clerks who process it. An account nobody closes keeps receiving its scheduled Imperium deposits indefinitely.

The local financial institution holding these accounts, call it something like the **Jorjor Provincial Trust**, quietly uses the accumulated float for ordinary banking purposes: loans, investments, covering shortfalls elsewhere. Nothing about this trips a normal audit, because each individual account still nominally balances; the fraud isn't in any one account, it's in the aggregate nobody's ever had reason to add up. It's a genuinely hidden channel of Imperium money, flowing into this specific town and quietly repurposed there, invisible by design rather than by conspiracy; nobody planned this, the bureaucracy just never built a mechanism that could notice it.

## 2.25: WHAT THE MONEY FUNDS, THE SHUTTER PROJECT

**Yes, this is exactly where a meaningful share of that float goes.** It is a small, informal, decades-old effort. Locals who know about it at all just call it **the Shutter Project**, and it is quietly funded through the body-bank float, attempting to study, reverse-engineer, or establish some working control over the ancient orbital platform's shutter mechanism (§2.16). No official budget line exists for it and never will; a project like this would never survive contact with a real Imperium funding review, which is exactly why it has to live inside a hidden channel instead of an approved one.

Consistent with Jorjor's TL7 baseline (Chapter 1, §1.4–5.1) even for its most ambitious secret project, picture the aesthetic as genuinely **analog, vacuum-tube-era radio and optical instrumentation** (home-built antenna arrays, hand-plotted orbital tracking, racks of dials rather than displays) scrappy 1950s-style electromagnetics research rather than anything that would read as anomalously advanced if stumbled upon. The people running it aren't a unified conspiracy with one motive: some genuinely believe they're protecting the world by quietly monitoring and probing it themselves rather than reporting it and risking Imperium seizure or exploitation; some see it as a path to real local power the Civil Service Bureaucracy would otherwise never grant them; a few are just true believers, drawn in by half-understood Ancients reverence more than by any practical plan.

## 2.26: THE PIT'S SHADOW, A MALIGNANT PSIONIC CORRUPTION

**Yes, as an optional lore layer a referee can choose to run with or ignore entirely.** Something in the Reserve's depths radiates a faint, chronic psionic corruption, not overt control, nothing anyone could point to or prove, just a slow, ambient dampening effect across the whole town: pervasive low-grade suspicion of outsiders, resistance to change or self-betterment, a kind of learned communal fatalism that outsiders read as small-town stubbornness and locals themselves would never think to name.

This fits Traveller's own setting texture well: psionics are officially suppressed and viewed with deep suspicion across most of the Imperium, so nobody in town would ever reach for that word even if they somehow understood what they were describing. They just call the population "tired," "set in their ways," or "not much for outsiders", and the bureaucracy, true to form, has no interest whatsoever in investigating something that would only generate more paperwork and unwelcome Imperium scrutiny if it were ever written down.

### Ambiance: the sound of the Feed Line

The slurry pipeline is buried and silent, but the machinery along it is not. Near the Terminal (Area 8) there is a constant low pump hum and a slow thump from the valves, felt through boots and truck frames more than heard, and it keeps going through the dust storm. At the motel it is only a faint throb in the floor or a glass of water on a quiet night (§2.7, result 8). Players will stop noticing it, which is the point: when the Feed Terminal stops pumping at Alert: Black (§2.28), the silence is a sound of its own. A PC who noticed the hum earlier gets that cue for free, and anyone else can catch it with an Average (8+) Recon check.

### Area 7: the Ranger Armoury

A hardened room at the base of the Tower, beside the Incident Room stairs. It exists because of the Y1113 Terrace disaster: after the Bureau lost a tour group, the Concession's charter obliged the Reserve to keep a **containment locker** for "a breach of the closed areas," stocked off the books with Imperial-issue equipment and never opened since. On the Reserve's forms it is "Equipment, Misc., Containment." It is the best-supplied room on Jorjor and it is entirely illegal for anyone but the Reserve's own staff (§2.32).

Two of the party can know about it before play. **Dabney** counted the Tower's armoured door and the rack in the ranger duty room on an earlier errand, and **Tomas** checked it on a pre-mission walk-through with a SolSec planner (Chapter 3, §3.3 and §3.7). **Gus Teller** (Chapter 4, §4.11) and **Ansel Voss** (Chapter 4, §4.6) also know it exists.

### Areas 12, 13, 14: the closed zone

**Area 12: The Terrace.** A broken shelf, three to ten metres above the Sink. The ground rings hollow underfoot. Dozens of **sinkholes** (Athletics (dexterity) or Recon check to walk safely; failure drops you 1D metres into a void with a warm, damp floor). Two surviving **tunnels** from the Y1113 collapse run toward the Sink; the best natural place to find *trapped* pickup members, alive but unable to climb out. Fluid seeps through the walls.

**Area 13: The Orchard.** The Egg-bloom grove: 30-odd plants 6–10 metres tall, spaced a few metres apart, closed like enormous eggs at midday and slowly opening at dusk. This is where the pickup pitched camp, and where the Mass took them. Captured crew members hang **inside open or half-open Egg-blooms** in a sedated, upright sleep. Their tents are still staked out, neatly, a few metres from the nearest bloom.

**Area 14: The Sink.** A breathing mat of tissue about a kilometre wide. Heat shimmer, the smell of a butcher's yard in summer, and a soft rhythmic *pulse* every few seconds that the body feels through the boots. A person can *lose* a pickup member here (wandering, hallucinating from the spores) but cannot easily *find* one. The mole's best approach is from below.

## 2.30: WHAT LIVES HERE, DRIFTERS, WADERS, EGG-BLOOMS

Everything alive on the surface is, one way or another, **a part of the Mass**. Four kinds matter at the table, and each has a size and a job. (Minis: use the sizes below. Drifters on flight stems or clear rods; Waders on tall legs; Egg-blooms as a large egg-shaped terrain piece with opening "petal" sections if you have them.)

### Where they are and what they're doing

| Area | Drifters (floating blobs) | Waders (long-legged blobs) | Egg-blooms (6–10 m egg plants) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | A few overhead at dusk. Tourists photograph them. | None | None |
| 3–4 | Hover over the steam, riding the thermals | **Feeding**: wading the slurry, picking at floating scraps | None |
| 5–6 | Rare | Rare (one or two at the lagoon edge) | None |
| 7 | **Watching**: a ring of Drifters circles the tower at dusk (rangers read them as an alarm) | None | None |
| 8 | A few; attracted by feed smell | **Waiting**: stand motionless at the docks as feed trucks unload | None |
| 9 | None | None | None |
| 10 | Some; follow the seep | Some; travel in pairs | None |
| 11 | **Grazing**: dense herds, hundreds, drifting with the wind | Scattered | A few young (3–5 m) |
| 12 | **Gathered**: low and tight above the sinkholes | **Patrolling** the rim: they have been *called in* | None |
| 13 | **Pollinating**: moving bloom to bloom | **Carrying**: ferrying wrapped bundles toward the blooms | **The Orchard**: 30+ at full size |
| 14 | Clustered thick; "sleeping" low on the mat | Few; stand in the mat like reeds | A handful at the edge |

![A Drifter](img/drifter.webp)

**Drifters (floating blobs).** Translucent, gas-filled bags 1–3 metres across, with trailing stinging filaments. They float on the basin's warm thermals and the evening wind and drift upwind in the day to seed spores. They are the Mass's **eyes**: a cluster of Drifters converging on a spot is the Reserve's alarm. They descend and cling to Waders and Egg-blooms in strong wind.

- **Hits 12 · Speed 3 m (drifts with the wind) · Armour 0**
- **Sting (filaments):** 1D damage; END 8+ or lose 1D DEX for 1D minutes (paralytic). Weapons that burst the bag release a **spore cloud** (1D rounds of reduced visibility; breathing without a filter means END check or hallucinations).
- **Behaviour:** ignore anyone coated in feed slurry (§7.3). Converge on gunfire, explosives, TL10+ emissions, or psionics.

![A Wader](img/wader.webp)

**Waders (long-legged blobs).** Stilt-legged blobs 3–5 metres tall, with a soft, bag-like body slung between four long legs and a cluster of grasping tendrils below. They graze slurry at the lagoon and **carry** things, wrapped bundles, including live people, to the Orchard. The Mass's hands.

- **Hits 40 · Speed 6 m · Armour 2**
- **Tendrils (grapple):** Melee 2, STR 12. A grappled victim is wrapped (STR check to break) and carried 6 m a round.
- **Stab (leg):** 3D damage, only against anything that has hurt them.
- **Behaviour:** slow and clumsy on boardwalk and uneven ground, **fast and sure across mud**. Do **not** attack anything smelling of feed slurry; they treat it as "part of the Mass." They are blind in a dust storm and hunt by smell and ground vibration (mole drilling attracts them).

![An Egg-bloom](img/egg-bloom.webp)

**Egg-blooms (egg-shaped flower plants, 6–10 m).** Tall, egg-shaped buds of overlapping petals on a thick, wet stalk. At midday they stay **closed**; at dusk the petals unfurl slowly like a flower opening in reverse, revealing a **cradle**; a deep, warm, nectar-filled pit lined with soft filaments. They are the Mass's **stomach and nursery**: they hold captured creatures in a sedated, upright sleep and sample them for days. Prey is not dissolved quickly; it is *kept*.

- **Hits 60 (6 m) to 120 (10 m) · Speed 0 · Armour 3 (petals), 0 (calyx at base)**
- **Embrace:** a petal sweep grapples anyone within 2 m (STR check to break).
- **Cradle:** anyone placed in or entering the cradle: END 8+ each round or fall into a sedated sleep (**helpless**, but alive and unhurt).
- **Pollen burst:** when struck hard, releases a cloud (as Drifter spore cloud) and a **shriek** audible for a kilometre (+1 AP).
- **Behaviour:** open to **warmth and light** at dusk, close fast in wind, dust, cold, or loud noise. In the dust storm (§2.31) every bloom **seals tight**, which makes cutting a victim out harder (Armour 5) but also makes the Orchard the quietest place in the Reserve.
- **Getting someone out:** open it with a bright, warm lamp (Routine check, 1D minutes, silent), or cut the calyx (Hits 25, **no AP**, but the bloom empties). Vibro-blades cut easily; slugs are slow.

![A Gutwing](img/gutwing.webp)

**Gutwings (the Mass's raiders).** Broad-winged flyers about four metres long with a six-metre span, slate-grey carapace, a hooked beak-skull, and a long jointed tail ending in a barbed fan. They roost in the Orchard (Area 13) and the Sink (Area 14) and almost never leave, but when the Mass is alarmed (as it was by the pickup at 16:20, Chapter 7, §7.5), it sends them to hunt whatever is warm, loud or emitting TL10+. The Mass's **talons**.

- **Hits 22 · Speed 12 m (flying; 3 m on the ground, clumsy) · Armour 2 (carapace)**
- **Skills:** Athletics (dexterity) 2, Melee (natural) 2, Recon 2, Survival 0.
- **Tail barb:** 3D damage, attack 2D + 3 vs 8+.
- **Bite:** 2D damage, only against a target it has already hit this round.
- **Dive:** a gutwing that flies 6 m or more in a straight line before its first attack of a fight adds 1D to the barb, and the target must make a Recon check (Average 8+) or be surprised.
- **Behaviour:** hunt by heat, light and TL10+ emissions. They **do not attack anything coated in feed slurry** (Chapter 7, §7.3). They break off when reduced to 8 Hits or fewer, or when half the flight is down, and circle back after 1D minutes. Floodlights (a truck's roof bar, headlights) dazzle: a gutwing lit at close range loses its next attack. A closed, dark, unheated room is invisible to them (Recon, Average 8+, to find a hidden group). They cannot get through a steel door, and a barb has to beat a vehicle's Armour.
- **Doors and glass at the motel (Chapter 5, §5.3):** a room door (hollow wood) is Armour 1, Structure 6. Window glass is Armour 0, Structure 2. The diner's steel back door is Armour 4, Structure 15.
- **Balance (the test the numbers were built for):** an unarmoured PC (about 20 Hits) goes down to two barbs. Cloth armour (Armour 8) cuts a barb to roughly 2 damage, so three vests change the fight. Against Armour 2, an Advanced combat rifle needs about three single hits or one burst (6D) to drop a gutwing, and a Gauss rifle (4D, AP 5) needs two shots. The pickup's door (Armour 1) stops nothing, but the Gravy Train's cab (Armour 4) shrugs off a barb except through the windscreen (Armour 1, Structure 3).
- **Number in the flight:** 1 to 5, set by what the party has. See Chapter 6, §6.4.

### Psionics

A character using **Telepathy** near the basin (even Life Detection) finds the Mass **looking back**: the whole basin registers as one vast, slow mind. The Mass notices telepaths: +1 AP, and Drifters turn toward them. Dr. Hale did exactly this at 16:20 today, by accident (Chapter 7, §7.5).

## 2.31: THE DUST STORM CLOCK

The storm established in §2.5 is a **haboob**, a wall of dust about a kilometre high rolling in from the south-west. It reaches the Reserve in stages.

*Read-aloud, when the table first sees it:* "You see it first as a bruise on the horizon, orange going to brown, a kilometre high. Then the light changes. The sky goes the colour of old rust, the shadows flatten, and the air goes still and heavy, like the world is holding its breath. The radio fills with static. Birds go quiet. Then the wall arrives. A low roar, and a hiss like a thousand hands rubbing sandpaper. Grit rattles the windows. The lights flicker. Everything tastes of copper and dust, and the world shrinks to the length of your arm."

| Stage | When | At the motel | At the Reserve |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1: Distant wall** | Act I (21:00–22:15) | Orange horizon, static on the radio | Tours cancelled. Drifters low. |
| **2: First gusts** | Act II (≈22:15–00:15) | Wind, grit, lights flicker | Visibility 50 m. Egg-blooms close. Gate shut except for manifests. |
| **3: Full storm** | Act III (≈00:15–05:00) | Highway closed. Visibility near zero. | Visibility 5 m. Waders blind. Egg-blooms sealed. **Surface patrols withdrawn.** Aircraft grounded. |
| **4: Passing** | ≈05:00–05:45 | Clearing sky | Dawn. Everything is visible again. |

**The late lighter.** At about **21:50**, during Stage 1, a low cargo lighter (a stub-winged atmospheric hauler, running lights dimmed) passes over the motel heading for the downport, far too low and too fast, and is not heard again. When Stage 3 grounds all aircraft it is the one craft still unaccounted for: the downport tower logs it as "diverted" and nobody will say where. It is the table's first clue that something is wrong at the Reserve, because its track bends toward the Feed Line Terminal first (Recon or Pilot 8+ to read the bearing). Its cargo and crew are the referee's call: Shutter Project staff, a Wei contact, or Pruitt's regional office sending in help that arrives too late. If the table follows it up in Act II, Del or Captain Pell (§4.12) can confirm a lighter set down at the Terminal pad before the storm shut the sky.

**The storm helps and hurts.** It hides the players from rangers and hides the rig's headlights; it also grinds filters, blinds sensors, chokes engines, and is a hard cap on how long anyone can be outside.

**Environmental hazards (Stage 3):**
- Atmosphere 4 (thin, tainted) *plus dust*: a filter is mandatory, and wears out in 4 hours (**Survival** to stretch it).
- Wind: Athletics (strength) to cross open ground carrying a load.
- Vehicles: Drive check each 30 minutes or stall.

## 2.32: THE LAW & TRAVELLERS

Jorjor is **Law Level 8**. Every ban at Law Level 8 and below applies to civilians and offworlders: all bladed weapons, stunners and visible armour (Law 8); shotguns (Law 7); all firearms (Law 6); personal concealable weapons (Law 5); light assault weapons and submachine guns (Law 4); military weapons (Law 3); portable energy and laser weapons (Law 2); explosives, poisons and undetectable weapons (Law 1). **Exempt:** the **Guarda Civil**, Jorjor's civil-service police (Cabo Marsh, Chapter 4, §4.5), Reserve rangers and Reserve security, and anyone holding a local permit. Two examples: the motel owner's farm shotgun (county permit framed behind the desk) and Tomas Reyes's pistol (a forged permit, Chapter 3, §3.7). Concealed armour is not banned until it is seen. Because the law is enforced through paper (§2.10), the usual consequence of a violation is a form, a fine, an impound or a hearing, not a gunfight.

**The SolSec guests and the Imperial pickup team have smuggled in whatever they carry.** Every weapon, charge and rifle in the cast is contraband unless this chapter says otherwise. Hidden gear is safe unless searched, which is why a visit from Cabo Marsh or Pruitt matters.

### Law enforcement checks

Roll 2D, add the DM, and if the total is **equal to or lower than the planet's Law Level (8)**, the Travellers are investigated or challenged by planetary law enforcement.

| Situation | DM | Response |
|---|---|---|
| First approach to a planet | +0 | Check |
| Offworlders wandering the streets of a city (once per day) | +0 | Check |
| Offworlders acting suspiciously | -1 | Check |
| Bar fight | -1 | Apprehended |
| Shots fired | -2 | Apprehended |
| Breaking and entering | -2 | Investigate |
| Firefight involving armoured Travellers and military weapons | -4 | Apprehended |
| Murder and carnage | -4 | Investigate |

- **Check:** travel documents and identities are checked by a police officer or guard, or electronically by querying the Travellers' comms. A successful **Admin** or **Streetwise** check allays suspicion. If it is failed, authorities move on to Investigation.
- **Investigate:** a detective or bureaucrat probes the Travellers' backgrounds. Searches, being followed, tapped communications and close questioning are all on the table. On Jorjor this is a **bureaucrat with a clipboard** (Chapter 2, §2.10).
- **Apprehended:** the police show up ready for a fight. Their response is proportional to the threat: batons, stunners and tranq gas for a bar brawl, the best weapons and armour at Jorjor's TL7 (or higher) for a firefight, with the Reserve's rangers close behind.

### Sentencing

Travellers arrested for a crime face punishment, rolled on **2D + DMs** on the Core Rulebook **Sentencing** table (not reproduced here). For smuggling banned goods, the DM is **the difference between Jorjor's Law Level (8) and the level at which the item is banned**:

| Contraband | Banned at Law | Sentencing DM on Jorjor |
|---|---|---|
| Bladed weapons, stunners, visible armour | 8 | +0 |
| Shotgun | 7 | +1 |
| Firearm (rifle, carbine) | 6 | +2 |
| Concealable pistol or revolver | 5 | +3 |
| Submachine gun, light assault weapon | 4 | +4 |
| Military weapon | 3 | +5 |
| Laser or portable energy weapon | 2 | +6 |
| Explosives, shaped charges, poisons | 1 | +7 |

### Where it comes up in this scenario

- **Cabo Marsh (Chapter 4, §4.5):** an approach to a motel with offworlders is a **Check** (2D+0, 8 or less). Offworlders acting suspiciously is -1. A failed Admin or Streetwise check turns it into an **Investigation**, and a search finds Tomas's pistol (+3 without the permit; a forged permit fails an Investigation, Chapter 3, §3.7) and any other gear.
- **The pickup team (Chapter 4, §4.10):** Okafor-Brandt's slug pistol (+3), Mbeki's shaped charges (+7) and Rook's slug rifle (+2) are all contraband. They landed in a sensor-shadowed wash precisely because the downport would have found them.
- **Using any of it on Jorjor:** shots fired is an **Apprehended** check at -2; an explosion or a firefight with armoured, military-weapon-carrying Travellers is -4. Explosives also cost +2 Alert Points on the Reserve (§2.28).
- **Reserve staff (Quill and the rangers):** exempt. They may shoot first and file the form later.
