## I.1 — WHAT THIS COVERS

Appendix G handles getting *to* the Pi Building — the water, the insertion methods, the exterior defenses. This appendix picks up once the team is inside, working up through the floors mapped in Chapter 2 toward Krein himself, and covers the practical question every table eventually asks: how do we get back out.

## I.2 — INTERIOR SECURITY, FLOOR BY FLOOR

Security isn't uniform through the building — it escalates the higher the team goes, and it escalates in a way that follows directly from what's already on each floor (Chapter 2's floor plans), not an arbitrary difficulty curve bolted on top.

| Floor | Expected Security | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — Ground** | Two lobby checkpoints (one per tower), light — a couple of guards each, ID/authorization checks that are running on reduced attention tonight. The Sentry Walker (Appendix G, §G.4) stands watch in the plaza between them. | Public-facing entrance, normally routine; tonight, undermanned like everything else on Ys |
| **1 — Records & Archives / Naval Liaison** | Minimal. Civilian administrative staff, no dedicated security presence, maybe an occasional roaming pair | Paper-pushing floor — nobody expected to need to defend it |
| **2 — Staff Offices / Meeting Rooms** | Minimal, same as Floor 1 | Same logic — ordinary office floor |
| **3 — Harbor Operations & Deterrent Control** | Light — naval technicians, not combat personnel, present to (nominally) staff the consoles Chapter 2's floor plan already shows mostly dark and unstaffed | These are operators, not guards, and most of the seats are already empty |
| **4 — Security Ready Room / Armory / ISS Liaison** | **Heavy — the real chokepoint.** This is the floor built for exactly this scenario: armed personnel, the armory, Vantry's own local liaison desk (Appendix C) | This floor's entire stated purpose is defending everything above it |
| **5 — Staff Barricade Position / Krein's Command Post** | Heavy, concentrated close around Krein specifically rather than spread across the floor | His own staff, making a last stand around the one person left to protect |

**The practical read for a table:** Floors 1-3 should move fast — a few tense checks, maybe a close call, but not a grinding fight. Floor 4 is where the mission gets hard, on purpose. Floor 5 is the finale.

## I.3 — WHERE'S KREIN?

**Floor 5, in his Command Post**, exactly where Chapter 2's fifth-floor plan puts him — the room is literally labeled that on the map. His Office & Comm Suite (with the hardened broadcast equipment from Appendix A, §A.4) and the Authorization Codex Vault sit adjacent, both accessible from the Command Post without crossing back through the Staff Barricade Position. He does not move from here during Act Three under normal circumstances — Appendix A is explicit that he can't be talked out of Ashfall, and there's no reason for him to abandon the one room in the building that still gives him control over anything. **One sensory detail worth using the moment a team enters his office:** it smells unmistakably of horseradish — his attaché case, and by extension the room itself (Appendix A, §A.4). It's a small, human, unplanned detail in a room otherwise built entirely around a terrible decision, and it's worth letting land before anyone starts searching.

## I.4 — DISABLING THE ELEVATORS

Each tower runs its own independent lift system, with a base-level maintenance interface near the Elevator Lobby shown on every floor of Chapter 2's plans. Disabling both is a genuine tactical option — not because it stops Floor 4's security from reaching Floor 5 (they're already stationed there), but because it's the team's best backup plan for keeping the **underground garrison** (Appendix G, §G.1) from reaching the upper floors at all, if the Pinnace Gambit's flood trick (§G.3) either wasn't used or didn't fully work.

**Task:** Electronics (computers) or Engineer 8+ (Average), one check per tower, roughly one minute each. Success locks that tower's lift wherever its cars currently sit — nobody rides it, in either direction, until it's manually overridden.

**The real tradeoff, and it's worth stating plainly at the table:** this cuts both ways. Denying the elevators to defenders also denies them to the team — from this point on, everyone's taking the stairs, both up to Krein and back down on the way out. That's a genuine strategic choice, not a free action: slower, more exposed movement for the team, traded against denying reinforcements a fast route to the fight.

**A quieter version of the flood trick, functionally:** where §G.3's Pinnace Gambit seals the garrison in with an environmental hazard (and needs the Pinnace itself as a resource), this is the cheap, low-drama backup — no crash, no wave, just two Electronics checks from inside the building. A team that skipped the Pinnace Gambit entirely, or wants a second layer of certainty, has this available.

## I.5 — PLANNING THE EXTRACTION: STOPPING PURSUIT

On the orbital strike idea specifically — I don't think it fits, and here's the actual reasoning rather than just a no: Team UNDERTOW's entire operational logic, established since Chapter 1 (§1.6), is that the Confederation will disavow them completely if this goes wrong, and their one real advantage is staying small enough and deniable enough that "disavow" is even a credible thing the Confederation could do. An orbital strike on an Imperial naval capital's own administrative tower isn't a tactic at that scale — it's an act of interstellar war, the single loudest, least deniable thing a six-to-nine-person infiltration cell could possibly trigger, and it would retroactively make every quiet, careful choice the team made all night pointless. It's a great action-movie beat for a different kind of story; it breaks this one.

**What actually fits the tone, and genuinely solves the same problem:**

- **A collapsed stairwell, Voss's way.** A small breaching charge (Chapter 2, §2.4) brought down behind the team as they retreat down a stairwell denies pursuit a path without denying it to anyone who wasn't already chasing them — surgical, not indiscriminate, and it's the character who'd actually think to do it.
- **The elevator lockout, repurposed.** If the team didn't already use §I.4 to keep the garrison out, using it on the way *down* — locking a tower's lift the moment they're past it — buys real separation from anyone still above them.
- **A deliberate second distraction.** Nothing stops the team from triggering a second, smaller diversion on the way out — a fire alarm, a comms jam, anything that reads as chaos rather than confirmed hostiles — to blunt Vantry's response without escalating the encounter track (Appendix C, §C.2) any further than it already has.
- **The water, again.** If Nerys is on the roster (Chapter 2, §2.8), exiting back into Meridian Sound the way they came is a pursuit-denial tool in its own right — nobody's chasing the team underwater as effectively as the team's own Dolphin specialist can move through it.
- **The rooftop pad, carefully.** Appendix G, §G.5 already covers this as an extraction option, including the "presidential signal" idea from this same session — a spoofed governor-level authorization buys a real grace period before pursuit organizes, though it's a clock, not a permanent pass.

**None of these need to be exclusive.** A genuinely well-planned extraction probably layers two or three of these — a collapsed stairwell behind them, then out through the water or the pad — rather than relying on one single trick to work perfectly.

## I.6 — HEAVY WEAPONS: LIMITS AND CONSEQUENCES

### Does the Plasma Weapon Have a Limit?

Threk's PGHP-14 (Chapter 2, §2.3) doesn't have an infinite ammo supply — man-portable plasma weapons run on a battery/fuel cell, not a conventional magazine, and CSC23's own real numbers back this up: even the far larger vehicle-scale Fighting Strongpoint (Appendix N) carries "1,100 shots" as an explicit, finite figure for its linked lasers, not an unlimited pool. Treat Threk's PGHP-14 the same way — a real but generous charge capacity (reasonable for a con-table session: enough shots that running dry mid-fight shouldn't come up unless a referee wants that specific complication), rather than a hard per-scene limit that needs tracking on a sheet.

**The consequence that actually matters is bigger than ammo, though.** Firing a plasma weapon is not a quiet, deniable act — it has a distinct thermal and radiological signature that any halfway-competent sensor operator in Meridian would immediately flag as **military-grade hardware**, not civilian or even standard security-issue gear. This should function as a hard escalation trigger on Vantry's track (Appendix C, §C.2):

- **A single plasma discharge**, used defensively in an already-compromised situation, is a Stage 3 (Authorized) trigger at minimum — same as any other confirmed hostile act.
- **Repeated or sustained plasma use** — treating it as a default problem-solving tool rather than a last resort — should read as **an unambiguous Stage 4 (Active Manhunt) trigger**, and a referee running this seriously should consider it grounds for Vantry escalating past her own ISS resources entirely: pulling in actual naval security or garrison reinforcement specifically because plasma signatures on a captive-government world during an active uprising reads as external military intervention, not local unrest. That's a genuine, in-fiction reason for "using it too much blows the operation's cover" beyond just the abstract escalation track — it's the kind of signature nobody in Meridian can plausibly ignore or explain away.

**Play this as a real tactical tradeoff, not a punishment.** Threk having a plasma weapon is a legitimate, powerful answer to serious threats (the Sentry Walker, Appendix N). Using it once when it matters is completely reasonable. Using it as a first resort against every problem is what should start drawing attention no amount of chaos in the streets can fully absorb.

### A Missile Launcher, For Taking Out a Second Robot

If the team is facing more than one heavy target (the Sentry Walker *and* something else — a second Walker, an unexpectedly reinforced position), a real CRB22 weapon fits cleanly: the **Rocket Launcher** — TL8, Range ~120m, Cr2,000, genuinely low-magazine (1 round on the base model, 2 rounds with the Scope/Smart variant), Blast trait, three Minor Actions to reload. That reload time effectively makes it a one-or-two-shot weapon in the middle of a fight exactly the way you're describing — a real answer to "one more heavy hit, then it's a rifle fight again," not an unlimited heavy-weapons option.

**Who carries it:** mechanically, Threk (Heavy Weapons (man portable) 3, Chapter 2, §2.3) is the clean skill fit — Rocket Launchers use the Heavy Weapons (portable) skill per CRB22. If the table wants it on a different character instead — Voss is a reasonable narrative fit given his general ordnance background even though Explosives isn't the actual skill this weapon calls for — that's a fine table-level swap; just note the character taking it should either have Heavy Weapons (portable) already or expect the standard unskilled penalty.

**One honest flag:** this weapon's exact damage figure was affected by the same PDF-extraction issue that's hit several other tables in this project — the Range (120m), TL (8), Cost (Cr2,000), and Magazine (1–2, confirmed) are all clean, solid numbers, but the Damage column didn't extract cleanly. Treat it as a genuinely serious one-shot hit — Blast-trait man-portable ordnance in Traveller is generally built to threaten vehicle- and robot-scale armor, which is exactly the role you're asking it to fill against a second Sentry Walker — and confirm the exact dice against your own copy before locking in a number.

## I.7 — WHEN THE CITY ITSELF BECOMES THE OBSTACLE: A TWO-STAGE STRUCTURE

This is a genuinely good structural idea, and it's worth building deliberately rather than as an improvised excuse. Here's a clean way to do it.

**The premise:** Meridian's traffic and transit infrastructure — already strained by a citywide uprising — degrades badly enough during or immediately after the Pi Building assault that neither flight (the rooftop pad, Appendix G §G.5, or any stolen air/grav asset) nor ground transport (the Reach-rated hauler, Chapter 3 §3.4, or anything else) can reliably get the team from the Pi Building to the Glass Reach in the same continuous push. This isn't a stretch — it's consistent with everything already established about Meridian tonight (Chapter 3's uprising-torn streets, Appendix C's ISS response mobilizing, loyalist checkpoints everywhere) escalating specifically *because* of what the team just did at the administration building.

**What this buys you, structurally:**

- **A natural act break.** The Pi Building assault (Appendix I, this whole document) becomes a complete, satisfying stage in its own right — urban, vertical, personal, ending in extraction rather than immediately chaining into the next objective.
- **A genuine tone shift for Act Two/Three.** The Glass Reach crossing (Chapter 5) and the bunker assault (Chapter 6) already read as a different kind of scenario — quieter, more desolate, post-apocalyptic-adjacent (the spike field, the thirty-one-year-old ruin, Appendix G's Halveth). Forcing a hard transition here, rather than a smooth continuation, actually serves that tonal shift instead of fighting it.
- **A legitimate reason to hand off to a second team**, if that's the specific structure a table wants — different characters, different session, maybe even a genuinely different group of players picking up at the Glass Reach with whatever intel/assets/complications the first team left behind (Basaran's information, the StudyDeck leads from Appendix D, the state of the Countdown clock, Vantry's escalation level). This mirrors how real convention/campaign play often works better as connected one-shots than one continuous marathon session.

**How to justify it in-fiction without it feeling arbitrary:** the same institutional chaos already established throughout this project is the natural cause — Vantry's response mobilizing city-wide (Appendix C), loyalist checkpoints multiplying in the aftermath of whatever happened at the Pi Building, ordinary traffic control breaking down as more of the garrison redeploys toward the administration district. A referee doesn't need to invent a new complication; they need to let the ones already on the page compound.

**If you want a hard trigger rather than "the referee decides":** tie it directly to the Appendix C escalation track — reaching **Stage 4 (Active Manhunt)** during or immediately after the Pi Building assault is a clean, mechanically-grounded reason for the referee to declare that reliable city-wide transport is no longer available to anyone, team included, for the remainder of the session. That gives the players a visible cause-and-effect: their own actions at the Pi Building are what closes this door, not an arbitrary GM fiat.

## I.9 — BADGE ACCESS CONTROL & THE DOOR TO KREIN'S OFFICE

Every loyalist security badge in the building grants routine floor and elevator access — nothing exotic, just a standard RFID/biometric token, the same kind of access-control layer any secure naval facility would run. What it does *not* grant, for anyone, is entry to Krein's own office door on Floor 5. That specific lock answers to exactly two badges in the entire building.

### Commander Alys Ferro — Security Commander, Sole Full-Access Holder

![Commander Alys Ferro](img/alys.webp)

*The only person in the building, Krein included, whose badge opens every door — a deliberate choice on Krein's part, made years ago, to have exactly one person he trusts completely with total physical access.*

Ferro carries visible scarring across one side of her face — old chemical or plasma burn scarring from a posting she doesn't discuss, the kind of injury that reads immediately as "this is someone who's already survived the worst version of her job once." She runs Floor 4's security operation personally rather than delegating it, and she's the reason that floor is a genuine chokepoint (Appendix I, §I.2) rather than just a name on a floor plan.

| | STR | DEX | END | INT | EDU | SOC |
|--|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Score | 10 | 9 | 10 | 8 | 9 | 7 |
| DM | +1 | +1 | +1 | +0 | +1 | +0 |

**Skills:** Gun Combat (slug) 3 · Tactics (military) 2 · Leadership 2 · Melee 0 · Melee (unarmed) 2 · Recon 2 · Electronics 0 · Electronics (security) 1 *(the "extra skillset" that sets her apart from the generic Loyalist Close Protection Detail, Appendix A §A.6 — she genuinely administers the badge system, not just carries a token)*
**Armour:** Combat Armour, Improved (**Protection +17, Rad 145**, TL12, Vacc Suit 0 required — CSC23)
**Weapon:** Advanced Combat Rifle (Range ~500m, standard published value, 3D, Auto 3, CRB22 p.125)

**Running her:** she is the single hardest personal obstacle between the team and Krein's door, by design — better armored, better trained, and better informed than anyone else on the floor. She should never feel like a reskinned generic guard. A referee wanting one memorable detail beyond her stats: she checks her own badge against the door reader out of habit, every single time, even though it's never once failed to recognize her.

**Neural.AI image prompt:** A woman in her 40s, powerfully built, short practical hair, one side of her face marked by old scarring — chemical or plasma burn scarring, healed but visibly textured, running from cheekbone toward the jaw. Wearing bulky combat armour, helmet off and tucked under one arm, a security badge visible on a lanyard or chest mount. Sharp, assessing expression — the look of someone constantly running threat calculations. Industrial harbor backdrop, warm firelight against cool blue dusk. Photorealistic, science fiction military portrait, no text, no logos.

### Sergeant Rourke — The Two-Gun Guard, Level 5 Hallway

![Sergeant Rourke](img/rourke.webp)

*Stationed in the corridor outside Krein's office. His badge is the second (and only other) one that opens the door.*

Rourke carries two Advanced Combat Rifles, one in each hand, and fires both together — a genuinely unusual loadout, and worth being upfront about the rules wrinkle: CRB22's Dual Weapons rule (p.78) is written for weapons "reasonably used in a single hand each," explicitly listing pistols and blades as the intended examples, not rifle-class arms. Treat Rourke as a deliberate, referee-invoked exception rather than a standard-legal build — his STR 12 and whatever Battle Dress-adjacent conditioning got him this posting are the in-fiction justification, and the rule itself explicitly reserves this kind of call for "some circumstances" at the referee's discretion.

| | STR | DEX | END | INT | EDU | SOC |
|--|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Score | 12 | 9 | 10 | 6 | 5 | 5 |
| DM | +2 | +1 | +1 | +0 | –1 | –1 |

**Skills:** Gun Combat 0 · Gun Combat (slug) 3 · Athletics 0 · Athletics (strength) 2 · Melee 0 · Melee (unarmed) 1 · Tactics (military) 1
**Armour:** Combat Armour, Advanced (**Protection +19, Rad 180**, TL14, Vacc Suit 0 required — CSC23) — genuinely heavier, Imperial Army-pattern gear rather than standard naval security issue, matching an unusual posting for a naval installation
**Weapons:** 2× Advanced Combat Rifle (Range ~500m, 3D, Auto 3 each, CRB22 p.125). **Dual Weapons rule (CRB22, p.78):** may attack with both rifles in the same combat round; cannot aim with either; –2 DM to both attack rolls while dual-wielding

**Running him:** he is not subtle and shouldn't need to be — Rourke's whole design is a wall of firepower in a narrow hallway, the kind of encounter where cover and positioning matter far more than a straight exchange of fire. His Advanced armor makes him meaningfully tougher than the generic Loyalist Detail (Appendix A, §A.6) or even Commander Ferro's Improved-tier gear — a team expecting a standard guard here is in for a genuine surprise.

**Neural.AI image prompt:** A heavily built man in his 30s, broad through the shoulders, wearing noticeably bulkier armour than a standard naval guard — Imperial Army-pattern rather than naval security issue, matte and heavily plated. Holding two advanced combat rifles, one in each hand, stance wide and braced for the recoil of firing both. Blunt, unremarkable face, minimal expression — a man built for exactly one job. Standing in a tight administrative hallway, warm interior lighting against the cooler tone of a corridor at night. Photorealistic, science fiction military portrait, no text, no logos.

**The badge objective, stated plainly for the table:** getting through Krein's door requires a badge from Ferro or Rourke specifically — nobody else's works, and no amount of generic Electronics or Security skill substitutes for actually having one of these two badges (or the codex from Appendix A, §A.4, if the team went after that instead). This is deliberately a two-person bottleneck, not a lock to be picked.
