# TRAVELLER: OPERATION ASHFALL
## Appendix D — Optional Side Act: Open Study, Closed Doors

*Referee Reference Document — If Time Permits*

## D.1 — THE GAP THIS FILLS

Chapter 6 lets the team find and override the control bunker. It never actually tells them **where the seven arrays' physical discharge points are** — the deep-buried charges themselves, scattered under Meridian and the other six coastal cities (Chapter 1, §1.4). The override sequence stops the *network* from triggering, but a table that stops to ask "wait, where are the other six bombs, actually?" deserves a real answer, not a hand-wave. This appendix is that answer, built as an optional bonus scene — use it if the table has time and wants one more sharp, clever beat before or during Act Three.

**Design inspiration, stated plainly:** this is adapted directly from a real 2021 Bellingcat investigation. Journalists found that US soldiers guarding NATO nuclear weapons sites had been using public online flashcard/study apps (Chegg, Quizlet, Cram) to memorize security procedures for their jobs — and in doing so, inadvertently posted publicly searchable cards revealing exact vault locations, camera counts, patrol schedules, and duress codes at six different European bases, some of it sitting exposed since 2013. Nobody hacked anything. Nobody stole a file. Junior personnel needed to pass a qualification test, used the easiest tool available, and never thought to check the privacy setting. It's a genuinely great real-world lesson in how institutional carelessness — the same kind Krein's whole governing style is built on (Appendix A) — leaks secrets that no amount of TL15 denial-network security can compensate for.

## D.2 — THE HOOK

Ys's naval technical staff — the people who actually maintain the Ashfall network's seven arrays, not the engineers who built it thirty-one years ago — have to periodically requalify on denial-system maintenance procedures. It's dense, technical, and boring to memorize by rote, so for years, junior techs have been doing exactly what junior techs do everywhere: building study flashcard sets on **StudyDeck**, a civilian planetary infonet service, and leaving them set to public by default because nobody reads the privacy settings on a study app.

Nobody built this as a leak. Nobody meant to expose anything. That's what makes it findable — and what makes it a satisfying discovery for a table that thinks to look for it rather than something they're handed.

**How the team learns this is possible at all:** Rivit (Chapter 2, §2.7) is the natural character to think of it — not because his corrupted memory helps, but because searching public civilian infrastructure for leaked technical data is exactly the kind of unglamorous, correct instinct a technical specialist has that the rest of the team might not. Alternatively, if Basaran (Chapter 3, §3.3) is still a live contact, he can mention offhand that "the young techs were always terrible about locking down their study decks" — a throwaway line that becomes the seed if a player picks it up.

## D.3 — RUNNING THE SEARCH

This should feel like real open-source investigation: patient, a little tedious, genuinely rewarding when it clicks — not a single lucky roll.

| Step | Check | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Realize StudyDeck is worth searching at all | No roll — this is the hook (§D.2), or an Electronics/Investigate 6+ if nobody's been pointed at it | Opens the rest of the scene |
| Find relevant public flashcard sets by searching terms known to be associated with Ashfall (array maintenance jargon, the network's internal designation, technician slang) | Investigate or Electronics (computers) 8+ | A handful of study sets turn up, made by named junior technicians, still public |
| Cross-reference the sets against what the team already knows to extract real coordinates | Investigate 8+ (Average), or automatic if the first check was a strong success (12+) | The team gets genuine, precise data — not just Meridian's array, but rough-to-precise locations for some or all of the other six |

**What the flashcards actually contain**, once found — worth reading a few aloud at the table for the same effect the real Bellingcat cards have: dry, procedural, and quietly horrifying in context. A card asking *"how many arming-cycle checkpoints on the Meridian array"* with a numbered answer. Another noting which of the seven arrays are already live versus still in cold storage as of the tech's last requal. One set includes an offhand note about a fault in the northernmost array's isolation relay — a detail nobody in Krein's office would ever have volunteered, and one the referee can use as a genuine mechanical assist (advantage on an Electronics check, or a free success on one Act Three override task, referee's call) if the team brings it to the bunker.

---

## D.4 — WHAT THIS ACTUALLY CHANGES

- **Precision:** the team goes from "the crater has a bunker" to knowing where all seven arrays physically sit — useful if a referee wants to run a version of Act Three where the team has to consider more than just Meridian's fate, or where warning the other six cities becomes a real (if probably unreachable-in-time) option the players have to grapple with, not just narrate around.
- **A found advantage, not a shortcut:** this doesn't disarm anything by itself. It's information, and it should feel like information — earned by patient, clever, unglamorous work, the same way it was in reality.
- **Tone payoff:** it's a quiet gut-punch that the deepest operational security failure on Ys wasn't Krein's decision, wasn't the uprising, wasn't even SolSec's infiltration — it was a handful of bored twenty-year-olds who didn't check a privacy toggle. That's worth letting land without comment.

**If the table doesn't have time for this:** skip it entirely. Nothing in Chapters 1–6 requires this appendix, and the core three-Act structure is complete without it.
