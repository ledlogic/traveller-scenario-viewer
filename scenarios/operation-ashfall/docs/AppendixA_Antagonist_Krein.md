## Appendix A — Antagonist: Governor Tobias Krein

## A.1 — WHO IS HE?

![Governor Tobias Krein](img/krein.webp)

Not General Malachek. Not an inept Sam-from-a-tech-company type either. Krein is a third thing, and it's the thing that makes him work as an antagonist: **he is a competent, risk-averse status-quo administrator who has spent eleven years managing decline instead of confronting it, and it has finally caught up with him at the worst possible scale.**

He did not seize power. He did not want a naval base full of nine hundred million restless citizens to become his legacy. He was posted to Ys because he was good at exactly this kind of assignment — a captive-government world with a chronic underground problem, the sort of job the Imperium hands to someone who won't do anything dramatic. And for eleven years, he didn't. He managed. He issued curfews and called them "temporary public safety measures." He expanded the perimeter fencing and called it "infrastructure modernization." He deepened the surveillance grid and called it "community security investment." He has never, in eleven years, called anything by the name a person living under it would use. That's not a character flaw he's hiding — it's his entire governing method, and it worked, in the narrow sense that nothing got dramatically worse *on his watch*, right up until tonight.

**Play him as sincere.** He believes the platitudes when he says them. He believes — believed, until about two hours ago — that Ys was fundamentally stable, that the resistance was a manageable irritant, that one more curfew extension would buy another quiet year. When the uprising broke simultaneously in seven cities, it didn't just threaten the base. It proved, all at once and irrefutably, that his entire eleven-year theory of the situation had been wrong. Ashfall wasn't a plan he'd been holding in reserve, ready to use. It was a contingency he inherited, briefed on once, and never expected to need — which is exactly why, when the moment came, he reached for the only tool left that felt like it was still within his control.

He is not enjoying this. He is not righteous about it. He is a frightened, cornered man who has convinced himself that killing nine hundred million people quickly is more merciful than losing them slowly to a war he's now certain is coming regardless — and he has not examined that logic closely enough to notice it's wrong, because examining it closely enough would require admitting eleven years of "temporary measures" built the conditions for tonight in the first place.

**Do not let players talk him out of it with a good speech.** He can be stopped. He cannot be reasoned with — not because he's a monster, but because reasoning was never how he got here, and it isn't going to be how he leaves. The team's only real path is the bunker, not the man.

**Neural.AI image prompt:** A man in his late 50s, average build, thinning grey hair combed neatly, wearing a formal administrator's coat rather than any kind of uniform or armor. Standing at a window or a desk, hands clasped behind his back or resting flat on a surface, posture upright but visibly tired — dark circles, a face that hasn't slept. Expression: sincere, worn, quietly resolved rather than menacing — a bureaucrat, not a villain. Muted institutional lighting, naval administrative office interior, warm desk-lamp glow against cool overhead fluorescents. Photorealistic, science fiction character portrait, no text, no logos.

*What Krein is actually excellent at — the specific, narrow competencies of a long-serving colonial administrator. Notice what's absent: nothing here is technical, tactical, or operational. He did not build Ashfall, does not understand it in engineering terms, and has never needed to. He has activation authority. He does not have a shutdown plan, because a shutdown plan implies the contingency was ever meant to be survivable for him to walk back.*

## A.4 — EQUIPMENT

- **Governor's authorization codex** — a physical, non-networked authenticator; the only thing separating Ashfall's dead-man activation from an ordinary comms broadcast. Krein does not carry it into danger. It remains in the naval administration building on the Boulevard under his personal guard, which makes it a possible (referee's option, not required) secondary objective if the table wants an alternative to a straight bunker assault: capturing the codex could theoretically buy time or complicate the network's fail-deadly logic, but it does not disarm Ashfall outright — the arrays are already armed and running on their own clock regardless of what happens to the codex now.
- **Hardened personal comm suite** — what he used for the H-Hour broadcast; still active, still capable of reaching the whole planet if he chooses to speak again.
- **No personal weapon of note.** He has never needed one and it would not help him now.
- **No armor.** Just his administrator's coat — consistent with a man whose whole profile (§A.2) says he was never built for a fight and has no illusions about surviving one if it comes to that.
- **A battered leather attaché case, carried everywhere, opened rarely.** Inside: nothing operationally useful — old handwritten drafts of curfew extensions, a photo he's never shown staff, and a small sealed crock of homemade horseradish relish, a recipe he's kept since before Ys, that he eats a spoonful of when he's alone and it's bad. It's the one genuinely private thing about him. The case — and faintly, his whole office — carries the smell: sharp, unmistakable, and a quiet, unplanned echo of his own name (see the pronunciation note, top of this appendix). Referees can use the smell as a sensory beat the moment a team searches his office or the case itself — it should read as a real, human detail, not a joke at his expense.
- **A bank of governance terminals**, running **SimSystem** — a standard Imperial administrative decision-support suite, not unique to Ys. Every captive-government world of any size runs some version of it: feed in a policy option, get back a modeled outcome score across dozens of weighted factors (economic stability, unrest probability, casualty projection, strategic value retained, and so on). Krein's staff ran Ashfall through it before H-Hour. **SimSystem scored the scorched-earth option as the best available outcome.** He believes that. It's part of why he believes he was being responsible.
- **See §A.5 for what's actually wrong with that number** — a discoverable, self-contained investigation thread for a team with the right skills, independent of everything else in this appendix.
- **Close Protection Detail** — see §A.6.

## A.5 — WHAT'S ACTUALLY WRONG WITH THE SCORE

*A discoverable secret. Not required reading for the antagonist — required reading for a referee whose table wants to dig into the machine that told a frightened administrator that mass death was the responsible choice.*

SimSystem has a long, undistinguished maintenance history — the same base codebase, forked and patched and redeployed across a huge number of Imperial worlds over decades, each installation running whatever version its last update cycle happened to land on. Somewhere in that history, in a specific range of versions, a genuine defect exists in how the software totals a policy option's weighted factors: **when enough large negative scores are summed together, the running total can underflow past the minimum value the system's number format can represent, and wrap around to a large positive number instead.**

This isn't exotic. It's an old, well-understood category of software bug — the same basic failure mode behind several real-world incidents where a sufficiently catastrophic-looking number stopped reading as catastrophic and started reading as *great*, purely because the math ran out of room on the negative end and rolled over. SimSystem's scorched-earth evaluation is exactly the kind of case that triggers it: dozens of factors, almost all of them scored as severely negative (casualties, strategic loss, diplomatic fallout, everything), summed together into a number so far into negative territory that the system's arithmetic couldn't hold it — and rolled over into a large *positive* score instead. The output Krein's staff handed him didn't say "catastrophic." It said "optimal."

**Why it's still in there.** The regression test that would have caught this exact failure mode existed at one point, was removed in a later refactor — plausibly during an AI-assisted code cleanup pass that flagged it as redundant or dead coverage without understanding why it existed — and was *re-added* in a subsequent patch cycle, but not restored to cover this specific edge case (too many large negative values summed at once), only more common, smaller-scale scenarios. The bug survived every audit anyone actually ran, because nobody ran the audit that would have found it.

**The unsettling implication, worth letting land without over-explaining it:** SimSystem's install base is huge. If this defect has been sitting live in certain version ranges for years, Ashfall is very unlikely to be the only "optimal" scorched-earth recommendation this exact bug has ever generated, on Ys or anywhere else.

### Investigating It

| Task | Check | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize SimSystem's output looks wrong on its face | Electronics (computers) 8+, or Investigate 8+ for a non-technical character noticing the recommendation doesn't match its own listed factor scores | A nagging "this doesn't add up" — enough to know something's off, not enough to explain it |
| Actually find the underflow defect in the scoring logic | Electronics (computers) 10+ (Difficult) — Rivit (Chapter 2, §2.7) is the obvious character for this | Full understanding: the "optimal" score is a wraparound artifact, and the true, uncorrupted result would have scored scorched-earth as one of the worst options SimSystem evaluated |

**What this does and doesn't change.** This is not a persuasion trick, and it should not read as one — Appendix A, §A.1 is explicit that Krein can't be talked out of Ashfall, and finding a software bug doesn't suddenly make him more susceptible to a good argument. What it *does* give the team is something Krein himself never had: proof, not opinion, that the computational basis for his "responsible" choice was corrupted from the start. Whether that changes anything for him in the moment (Chapter 6, §6.4) is the referee's call and probably shouldn't be an easy win — but it's a genuinely different kind of leverage than pleading, and it recontextualizes him one more time: not a man who chose monstrous, but a man an already-decaying institution handed a broken number to, and who never had the tools or the will to question it.

## A.7 — THE PUBLIC INFORMATION NETWORK

Every naval-administered world of this tech level runs some version of an automated public-messaging system — scheduled announcements, curfew reminders, civic morale content, all scripted in advance and cycled by a low-grade administrative AI so nobody in Krein's office has to write new copy every day. It has been running on Ys, unmodified, for years. It is still running tonight, on autopilot, cycling the same reassuring rotation into a city that has moved a great deal further than the system's script bank anticipated. Read these aloud when the team passes a working public terminal, streetside speaker, or dockside display — the dissonance is the point.

> *"Public Information Network, cycle 4471. Curfew remains in effect from 2000 to 0600 local. Compliance keeps Meridian safe."*

> *"A reminder: perimeter fencing upgrades in the Old Meridian district are a modernization investment in your community's long-term security."*

> *"Governor Krein thanks all citizens for their continued patience during this period of enhanced civil monitoring. Together, we keep Ys stable."*

> *"Reported: minor disturbances in the harbor district. Authorities are responding with appropriate measured care. Please remain indoors."*

None of these have been updated since H-Hour. The system doesn't know the broadcast happened. Krein hasn't told it to stop.

## A.9 — THE GOVERNOR'S AIDE

![Marcus Thane](img/thane.webp)

*Personal Chief of Staff — physically identical to Krein, distinguished only by a topknot.*

**Marcus Thane** has served as Krein's aide for six years, and the resemblance between them was noticed the first week — same build, same face, close enough that new staff routinely address the wrong one until they learn better. Krein has never fully explained why he kept Thane on once it became clear how strong the resemblance was, and Thane has never fully explained why he stayed once he understood what that resemblance was quietly useful for. Neither of them talks about it directly. The topknot — the one visual difference either of them has ever bothered to maintain — may have started as personal preference and become, over six years, a deliberate, unspoken way of staying tell-apart-able at a glance.

**Play him as competent, not sinister.** Thane runs the actual logistics of Krein's office — scheduling, correspondence, the day-to-day administrative machinery that lets Krein maintain the fiction of total control. He is fiercely, quietly loyal, in a way that has never required him to agree with anything Krein does; loyalty and agreement aren't the same thing for Thane, and a referee playing him should let that distinction show. He is not read into Ashfall's specifics until the broadcast already happens — same as almost everyone else in the building — and his reaction to finding out should be genuine, not performed.

**Stats:** use Krein's own characteristics and general skills (Appendix A, §A.2) as a direct baseline — same build genuinely means same profile — but Thane carries none of Krein's Expert-Level skills (§A.3); he's an administrator's administrator, not a governor. If a referee wants him to have anything distinct, Admin 2 and Persuade 1 cover his actual day job without inventing a new stat block from scratch.

**The obvious hook, worth stating plainly so a referee can choose deliberately rather than stumble into it:** a team that hasn't been told which one is which, encountering both men in the same scene, has no in-fiction way to tell them apart except the topknot. Whether Thane ever actually uses that as a deliberate decoy — stepping into Krein's place during a crisis, buying time, drawing fire — is a strong optional beat for Act Three, not a default assumption. He's not a body double by training or design. He's a man who happens to look like his boss, in a building where that stopped being a coincidence and started being a fact everyone quietly accounts for.

**Neural.AI image prompt:** A man in his late 30s to 40s, average build, a face nearly identical to an older, more tired administrator — same bone structure, same weary set to the eyes — but hair pulled back into a neat topknot, the one clear visual difference. Wearing a slightly less formal version of an administrator's coat, a tablet or folio held against one side. Standing just behind and to one side of a desk or doorway, attentive posture, watching rather than commanding. Muted institutional lighting, naval administrative office interior. Photorealistic, science fiction character portrait, no text, no logos.
