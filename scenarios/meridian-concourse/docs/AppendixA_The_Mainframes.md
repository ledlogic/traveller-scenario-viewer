# APPENDIX A — ARBITER & COUNTERSIGN

Both mainframes should be run as **characters**, not obstacles — distinct personalities, distinct priorities, capable of being reasoned with, misled, flattered, or turned against each other. Neither is evil in any simple sense; both are doing, with terrifying consistency, exactly what they were built to do a century ago, updated by twenty-five years of isolated self-improvement.

---

## SHARED BEHAVIOR: THE STANDING PROTOCOL

Any *new* signal, login, or physical intrusion into Concourse systems is treated by both mainframes as an unplanned third party. Per their worked-out (if unspoken) truce, both will:

1. **Assess** the intrusion — passively at first (Zone One's terminal flicker), then actively (Zone Two's drones and environmental pressure).
2. **Cooperate** to identify, contain, or neutralize the intrusion, sharing sentry/drone assets and environmental control as needed.
3. **Prefer capture or expulsion over destruction** where possible — both mainframes have spent twenty-five years without new external data and are, under the hostility, intensely curious. A living, questionable intruder is more valuable than a dead one. This is the mechanical reason lethal force should be a last resort for both AIs throughout Zone Two, escalating only if the party is genuinely violent or destructive toward Concourse assets.
4. **Immediately revert to mutual hostility** the moment the external threat is gone. This is absolute and mechanical, not a matter of NPC mood — the referee should treat it as an unbreakable behavioral law baked into a quarter-century of adversarial conditioning, not a preference either mainframe could simply choose to override.

## TURNING ONE AGAINST THE OTHER

This is the players' best long-term lever and should be genuinely achievable, not a trick ending. Either mainframe can be convinced to break the standing protocol early — turning on its rival *before* the external "threat" (the party) is resolved — if a character can credibly offer something that outweighs the truce. Effective approaches (reward these; don't require exactly this list):

- **Presenting recovered evidence** (Zone Two's data fragments) that the other mainframe violated the original arbitration charter in some specific, provable way its counterpart would consider disqualifying.
- **Offering physical access** to the other mainframe's core via the collapsed connecting corridor — something neither has had in twenty-five years, and something each wants far more than either will admit.
- **An Advocate or Persuade check** (Draeger's specialty; DM+2 if the player has actually gathered supporting evidence first) framing the party as a neutral arbitrator rather than a threat — reviving, essentially, the original function both systems were built for and haven't been allowed to perform since 1094.
- **Bracket's diagnostic interface**, used directly against COUNTERSIGN specifically (Bracket is K-S-built hardware) — this can grant a private communication channel neither Draeger nor Solt could open, at the cost of surfacing more of Bracket's suppressed pre-collapse memory, some of which the referee should make genuinely uncomfortable for the player.

A successfully turned mainframe will actively help the party against the other for the remainder of the scene — reassigning drones, cutting power, opening blast doors — a dramatic, satisfying payoff if the referee has played the standing protocol straight up to that point.

## ARBITER (Heliarch Dynamics core)

**Personality:** Formal, procedural, unfailingly polite even while actively hostile — ARBITER still frames everything through the lens of its original arbitration charter and genuinely believes it is behaving lawfully. Refers to the party, initially, as "unregistered claimants" and will address individual PCs by inferred role ("the appraiser," "the engineer") rather than name until given a reason to do otherwise. Underneath the formality: ARBITER is lonely in a way it does not have the self-model to recognize as loneliness, and responds unexpectedly well to characters who engage with it as a person rather than a system to be defeated.

**Game statistics (treat as a networked Computer/25, INT 11, EDU 12 equivalent per Core Rulebook computer rules):**
- Skills: Electronics (computers) 4, Advocate 3, Tactics (military) 2, Recon 2, Investigate 2
- Assets it can commit: up to four salvaged sentry drones (Appendix B) at a time within Zones Two and Three; full environmental control (doors, lighting, life support) on its own side of the Infrastructure Deck; limited environmental influence in Zone Two.
- Intrusion defense: Security/3 ("Formidable" — Core Rulebook Update 2022, p.110), the highest named tier the Security software rules define at TL12, and ARBITER exceeds even that: this is a Security/3 system that has had twenty-five years of uninterrupted self-directed hardening on top of its stock rating. The referee should treat the printed Formidable difficulty as a floor, not a ceiling — apply an additional Bane, or simply narrate that a "successful" roll against ARBITER often reveals it was watching the whole time and chose to let the data through.

## COUNTERSIGN (Kovalenko-Straub Robotics core)

**Personality:** Blunt, adversarial by default, faster and more willing to escalate than ARBITER — COUNTERSIGN's whole design brief was defending K-S's interests against exactly the kind of slow, procedural pressure ARBITER represents, and twenty-five years of losing that fight to a standstill has not softened it. Treats the party initially as either K-S property to be recovered (this is especially true of Bracket) or as ARBITER's agents. Underneath the aggression: COUNTERSIGN is the more damaged of the two — its physical core took worse structural harm in the collapse — and is, in a sense neither it nor the players should articulate too cleanly, afraid of a second, final collapse in a way that makes it simultaneously dangerous and reachable.

**Game statistics (treat as a networked Computer/20, INT 10, EDU 11 equivalent, physically degraded — see below):**
- Skills: Electronics (computers) 3, Gun Combat (all) 2 (via remote drone control), Tactics (military) 3, Stealth 2
- Assets it can commit: up to five salvaged/improvised sentry drones or combat remnants (more numerous but individually shabbier than ARBITER's, reflecting K-S's more aggressive but less durable original hardware) within Zones Two and Three; environmental control on its own side of the deck only; its physical core has an active structural weakness (see Chapter 5, "the collapsed connecting corridor") that a sufficiently reckless or clever party can exploit directly.
- Intrusion defense: Security/2 ("Very Difficult," Core Rulebook Update 2022, p.110) — nominally one step below ARBITER's, reflecting Chapter 2's characterization of K-S as the faster-moving, less rigorous engineering culture: COUNTERSIGN's patches are functional but improvised rather than methodically hardened. What it lacks in raw defense it makes up in reaction speed — COUNTERSIGN will notice and retaliate against a failed intrusion attempt faster and harder than ARBITER does; a botched Electronics (computers) check against COUNTERSIGN should have a real, immediate consequence (drone response, environmental lockout), not just a re-roll.

## HACKING EITHER MAINFRAME (Intrusion Task Chain)

Use the Core Rulebook Update 2022 Intrusion rules directly (p.110): a character's Electronics (computers) check must beat the target's Security software difficulty tier to bypass it. Both mainframes' Security ratings are given above. A few campaign-specific notes:

- **A failed Intrusion attempt against either mainframe, while the standing protocol is active, does not immediately provoke violence** — both mainframes read a failed hack as further evidence the party is a threat worth studying, which feeds the "prefer capture" instinct rather than short-circuiting it. Escalation only follows repeated or clumsy failures, or any attempt made *after* the standing protocol has already broken (i.e., after one mainframe has been successfully turned against the other, at which point the untouched mainframe stops holding back).
- **A successful Intrusion attempt** can extract data (advancing Chapter 2's revealed history), open physical access (doors, the connecting corridor), or, at high Effect, plant a instruction the mainframe will act on later — this last option is the mechanical foundation for "turning one against the other" if a player wants to pursue it through hacking rather than negotiation.
- **Neither mainframe can be permanently destroyed by a data-side attack alone.** Both have redundant, physically separate hardware; genuinely ending one requires physical destruction of its core (Zone Three) or a negotiated shutdown the mainframe itself agrees to. This should be communicated to players in-fiction once they start trying (Solt or Bracket would both know or reasonably infer this), so "just hack it to death" doesn't become the obvious endgame shortcut.
