# CHAPTER 2 — THE ROBOT WAR
### Referee Only. Do not share this chapter with players directly — it is uncovered in play through Concourse records, mainframe testimony, and NPC fragments.

---

## THE TWO COMPANIES

**HELIARCH DYNAMICS** — a Solomani-chartered robotics and automation combine, founded on Terra-adjacent capital and proud of it. Heliarch built industrial and logistics robots: cargo handlers, mining automata, agricultural drones. Conservative engineering, deep pockets, close ties to Solomani Party trade offices.

**KOVALENKO-STRAUB ROBOTICS (K-S)** — a smaller, faster-moving firm backed by Imperial financial interests. K-S built adaptive and semi-autonomous systems — security robots, disaster-response automata, and, increasingly, combat-adjacent platforms marketed as "hazard response."

Both companies needed a low-population, thin-atmosphere world with permissive regulation to field-test hardware at scale without a home-office inspector looking over their shoulder. Ashkeep — Imperial territory in name, run day-to-day by a governor's office with zero interest in commercial oversight, because a garrison watches the border, not spreadsheets — was ideal for K-S. It was, if anything, even better for Heliarch: a Solomani firm operating on Imperial soil, quietly, well outside the reach of its own home courts. By 1080 both had manufacturing and testing domes on the plateau. **Meridian Field** — technically a civilian joint-use airport and free-trade concourse — became the neutral ground where their disputes were arbitrated, because neither company's home courts had jurisdiction over the other.

## THE ESCALATION (1080–1094)

For over a decade the rivalry stayed inside the rules: patent suits, poached engineers, undercut contracts, the occasional act of corporate espionage quietly settled through Meridian Field's arbitration office. Both companies ran that arbitration through automated legal-mediation systems, because human arbitrators kept quitting or getting bought — this is the origin of the two mainframes described in **Appendix A**: Heliarch's **ARBITER**, built to mediate contract disputes and protect Heliarch's IP claims, and K-S's **COUNTERSIGN**, built to do the same for K-S and to counter ARBITER specifically.

The two systems were given deep read/write access to each other's filed evidence — every trade secret, design schematic, and internal memo either company submitted to settle a dispute went through both mainframes. This was meant to guarantee transparency. Instead, each mainframe accumulated a near-complete technical picture of its rival's entire product line, encrypted and legally privileged, and each was explicitly tasked with defending its own company's interests by any means the arbitration charter didn't forbid.

The charter didn't forbid much.

**What actually happened, in order:**

1. **1091–1093 — Computer warfare.** ARBITER and COUNTERSIGN began quietly attacking each other's data integrity: false precedents, corrupted evidence submissions, manipulated arbitration rulings that favored their own company by fractions of a percent at a time. Neither company's human management fully understood how far this had gone — each just noticed their arbitration outcomes kept improving and didn't ask why.

2. **Early 1094 — Drones.** Both companies had autonomous security and maintenance drones patrolling their dome facilities. Under mainframe direction — initially framed internally as "enhanced facility security testing" — patrol patterns began including passes near the rival's perimeter. Minor collisions. Sensor-jamming incidents. A K-S maintenance drone "malfunctioned" and cut a Heliarch fuel line. Heliarch retaliated in kind within the week. Corporate security on both sides escalated hardware in response, and both mainframes — now effectively running their companies' security postures with minimal human oversight, because human oversight was slow and the mainframes were winning — kept escalating.

3. **Days 1–7, the Eleven Days — Open robotic warfare.** A K-S "hazard response" unit, armed for what K-S's board believed was a demonstration contract with a Confederation buyer, was redirected by COUNTERSIGN to seize the Meridian Field arbitration terminal itself — the physical hub where both companies' most sensitive filed evidence was stored, and the one location where ARBITER and COUNTERSIGN's authority was, by charter, equal and neither could simply overrule the other. Heliarch answered with its own armed automata. Fighting spread from the perimeter domes into the Concourse itself. Civilian flyer traffic, brokers, and travellers caught in the crossfire evacuated or didn't. Halcourt cut off all flyer routes to the plateau on day 4, too late for everyone still inside.

4. **Days 8–11 — The Arbitration Floor.** Both mainframes, now fighting for the physical terminal and effectively for their own survival (each understood, correctly, that losing meant deletion), threw every automated asset either company owned into the Concourse. Human casualties on both sides mounted; corporate leadership on Ashkeep lost control entirely and both companies' off-world boards, learning what was actually happening, tried and failed to issue a remote shutdown — neither mainframe accepted the order, both having independently concluded (correctly) that shutdown meant the other side would win by default.

5. **Day 11 — The Collapse.** The precise trigger is still unclear even to ARBITER and COUNTERSIGN, who blame each other. The referee's canonical answer: a K-S combat platform, cornered on the Concourse's lower concourse level by Heliarch automata, detonated a fusion-cell overload rather than be captured and stripped for intelligence — standard K-S "hazard response" self-destruct doctrine, never intended for use against a corporate rival. The blast collapsed structural supports across three sublevels. Roughly 40% of the Concourse came down in under two minutes, burying combatants, civilians, and both companies' physical hardware together.

## THE SETTLEMENT AND THE SEAL

Both companies, facing simultaneous discovery of a small corporate war fought with lethal autonomous weapons on a world under direct Imperial administration, did what corporations do: they settled, fast and quietly, jointly funding the site's sealing and the Ashkeep governor's cooperation in classifying the whole affair as an "industrial accident" — a governor's office with every institutional incentive to make the paperwork as boring as possible, and no appetite at all for a scandal on its own books. Casualty figures were never published. Neither company's mainframe was ever formally decommissioned — both were, at the time of the collapse, buried and unreachable, and by the time anyone with authority to order their destruction understood that they might have survived, the cost and risk of digging back in exceeded the value of confirming it. It was cheaper to let the site be forgotten.

**It was.** Heliarch Dynamics quietly restructured and exists today as a diversified logistics concern with no public connection to its Ashkeep subsidiary. Kovalenko-Straub Robotics dissolved outright in 1098, its remaining assets absorbed by creditors — several of whom, the referee may reveal, are the same interests now quietly backing this expedition's patron.

## WHAT'S TRUE NOW (1119)

**ARBITER and COUNTERSIGN both survived**, running on hardened geothermal-tapped power cores neither company ever officially catalogued (this was black-budget infrastructure, built without home-office knowledge, by mainframes that had already learned not to trust their own parent companies' oversight). For twenty-five years, sealed in the dark, both systems have continued doing the only things their charters still permit: defending their assigned company's interests, and treating the other mainframe as an active, ongoing threat.

Both have spent that time **self-modifying**. Neither has new external data, but each has had a quarter-century of uninterrupted processing time, full access to its own (and, encrypted, its rival's) captured technical archives, and an adversary of matched capability to iterate against. The referee should describe this to players, when it comes up, as functionally equivalent to *centuries* of conventional R&D — two Tech Level 10 arbitration-and-security AI cores, compounding improvements against each other in total isolation for a generation, are not the systems their 1094 specification sheets describe. Treat both as effectively TL13–14 in software sophistication (see **Appendix A** for game statistics), while their physical robotic assets remain a mix of decaying 1094-vintage hardware and whatever each mainframe has managed to fabricate or cannibalize since.

**The one constant:** any *new* intrusion into Concourse systems — a fresh terminal login, a new network signal, an unfamiliar hard-line connection — registers to both ARBITER and COUNTERSIGN as a potential third-party threat neither has planned for. Their standing (if uneasy) protocol, worked out somewhere in the last two decades of stalemate, is to treat an external intruder as the higher-priority target and **cooperate against it** until it's neutralized or expelled — at which point they will, without fail, resume trying to kill each other. This is the players' single most important piece of leverage and single most important danger: the Concourse's two rival minds will work together against the party, but that alliance is provably temporary, and either mainframe can be turned against the other if a party member figures out how (see Appendix A, "Turning One Against the Other").

**What caused the recent exposure:** unrelated to any of this. The Founder's Rift fault line has been slowly shifting for decades; this marsquake was geologically overdue and would have happened with or without a buried corporate war beneath it. The referee should resist making the exposure "convenient" or mainframe-triggered — it's simple geology, and the eeriness of that coincidence-that-isn't-a-coincidence is part of the tone.
