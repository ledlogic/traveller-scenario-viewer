# GASHIDDA BESTIARY
### Fauna of the Equatorial Reef Shelf and Atmospheric Biosphere
*A Xenobiological Field Reference for Mongoose Traveller 2nd Edition*

---

**World:** Gashidda (UWP: A56A969-E) · Dingir Subsector · Solomani Rim  
**Environment:** Cool water world; equatorial reef shelf (depth 20m–4,000m); lower atmosphere (0–12km)  
**Compiled by:** Makara Arcology Marine Science Institute, Imperial Survey Branch  
**Classification standard:** Third Imperium Xenobiological Registry (TIXReg)

---

> *The equatorial seas of Gashidda are among the most biologically productive ocean systems in the Dingir subsector. The atmosphere above them is not empty. Colonisation and military occupation have left the deep reef largely unstudied and the atmospheric biosphere almost entirely uncharacterised. What follows represents current knowledge — which is to say, a fraction of what lives here.*

---

## ECOLOGICAL OVERVIEW

Gashidda's biosphere operates across two domains that most inhabited worlds treat as entirely separate: the ocean and the sky. On Gashidda they are connected. The same biological productivity that drives the deep reef ecosystem generates the atmospheric layer of filamentous microorganisms — the Driftweb — that gives the sky its characteristic grey-green colour and filters the already-dim light of the G8 IV primary into something dimmer still.

Gashidda's oceans are cold, oxygenated, and extraordinarily rich. The equatorial reef shelf — the same geological formation on which Makara and the other undersea arcologies are built — supports a layered ecosystem running from sunlit shallows down to abyssal trench systems at 6,000+ metres. The reef itself is a living structure: a composite organism of bioluminescent colonial polyps, mineral-secreting filter feeders, and chemosynthetic bacteria that convert deep geothermal vents into biological energy.

The dominant colour palette of Gashidda's reef is **blue-green bioluminescence** against absolute darkness, punctuated by the amber and orange of vent-adjacent thermal communities and the pale silver of open-water predators reflecting whatever light exists. Visitors descending from the arcologies into external observation bays frequently describe the experience as looking into a living sky — the reef columns rising like inverted mountains, trailing light.

Four ecological zones are relevant to this bestiary:

| Zone | Altitude/Depth | Light | Character |
|------|---------------|-------|-----------|
| **Atmospheric Layer** | 2–12km altitude | Filtered starlight | Filamentous microorganism suspension; Driftweb |
| **Shelf Shallows** | 0–200m depth | Filtered surface light | Dense, competitive, diverse; most food production |
| **Mid-Shelf** | 200–1,500m depth | Bioluminescent only | The primary reef column ecosystem; apex predators |
| **Deep Vent Fields** | 1,500m–4,000m depth | None (chemosynthetic) | Sparse, ancient lineages; poorly understood |

## SPECIES ENTRIES

### 00 · DRIFTWEB
*Gashidda Atmospheric Filament Algae · Filamentophyta atmospherica gashiddana*

**Ecological Zone:** Lower and mid atmosphere (2–12km altitude)  
**Size:** Individual filaments 0.1–4mm diameter, 2–80cm length; mat structures up to several metres across  
**Behaviour:** Passive suspension; photosynthetic; colonial aggregation in thermal updraft zones  
**Threat Level:** None (non-motile microorganism)  
**Commercial Value:** ★★★★☆ — Emerging biofuel feedstock; significant industrial interest at TL E

---

**Description:**

The Driftweb is the most visible component of Gashidda's atmospheric biosphere and the organism most directly responsible for the world's characteristic grey-green sky. It is not a single species but a complex of related filamentous photosynthetic microorganisms — cyanobacteria-analogs and single-celled algae producing long-chain polymer strands — that collectively form a semi-continuous biological layer in Gashidda's lower and middle atmosphere.

Individual Driftweb filaments are translucent to pale green, 0.1–4mm in diameter and ranging from a centimetre to nearly a metre in length. Under magnification they are visibly segmented, with regular nodes where the organism produces lipid-rich reproductive bodies — small amber beads visible to the naked eye in dense concentrations. The filaments are slightly charged, causing them to orient loosely along atmospheric electrical field lines and aggregate into the mat structures visible from the surface as the characteristic grey-green overcast. In direct light — when breaks in the cloud cover allow the star to illuminate a section of the Driftweb layer — the mats appear as slow-moving silver-green veils, sometimes trailing individual long filaments below them like the undersides of jellyfish.

The Driftweb's biological relationship to the ocean ecosystem below is not yet fully characterised, but the working hypothesis of the Makara Marine Science Institute is that it represents an extension of the ocean biosphere into the atmosphere rather than an independent ecosystem. Updraft currents carry spores and fragments from wave-surface foam into the lower atmosphere, where the organisms reproduce and form aggregations; precipitation eventually returns biological material to the ocean, where it enters the reef food chain. The entire system functions as a planetary-scale pump, fixing atmospheric carbon through photosynthesis and delivering organic material to the ocean below.

**The lipid fraction:** Driftweb filaments are approximately 18–24% lipid by dry weight — a consequence of the organisms' need to maintain structural integrity and buoyancy in the atmospheric layer. This lipid fraction is chemically similar to medium-chain triglyceride oils used in standard biofuel production and can be processed into a usable aviation and atmospheric fuel feedstock using TL 9+ biorefinery equipment. At TL E, onboard biocracker units capable of processing harvested Driftweb material in real time during flight are entirely feasible.

**Scoop-jet harvesting:** The logical exploitation model at TL E involves atmospheric vehicles fitted with wide-mouth intake scoops that collect Driftweb material during flight through the 4–8km altitude band where concentrations are highest, feeding it to onboard bioprocessing units that crack the lipids into fuel and discharge the remaining biological matter (primarily the cellulose-analog structural polymer, which is biologically inert) as a fine mist. A sufficiently efficient scoop-jet skimmer can theoretically achieve energy-neutral or net-positive fuel production on long atmospheric transits over Gashidda — harvesting more fuel from the Driftweb than it consumes flying through it.

The technology exists and is understood. What does not yet exist is commercial infrastructure at scale. The Makara Marine Science Institute has published preliminary yield data. Ling Standard Products has filed three provisional patents on Gashidda-specific biorefinery designs. The Seaharvester LIC business development team has produced an internal feasibility assessment describing Driftweb harvesting as "the single most underexploited economic opportunity on Gashidda." The Imperial military administration has not yet regulated the atmospheric harvesting sector because it has not yet understood that it needs to. This window will not remain open indefinitely.

**Political and tactical dimensions:** The Driftweb layer is difficult to monitor. Conventional ship and vehicle traffic sensors lose resolution in the atmospheric algae layer, particularly at 6–10km altitude where the mat density is highest. A scoop-jet skimmer operating in that band — legitimately harvesting Driftweb or otherwise — is substantially harder to track on sensors than a vehicle operating in clear air. This is not lost on the resistance networks. The practical range of scoop-jet vehicles between Gashidda's scattered islands, flying through the Driftweb rather than above it, is a surveillance gap the Imperial Army has identified but not yet resolved.

**Vanthara Truth significance:** The Driftweb has been incorporated into Vanthara Truth theology with a directness that surprised Imperial cultural analysts when they first encountered it in movement literature. The theological argument is elegant: the Driftweb breathes the same atmosphere as Gashidda's people. It is sustained by the same star — a distant, cool echo of the First Sky's sun, but the same category of thing. When practitioners speak of *breathing what Terra breathed* (the Second Breath theology), the Driftweb is the visible proof that the atmosphere itself is alive and participating. To breathe filtered, arcology-processed air — air that has passed through the same biological layer as the Driftweb — is to participate in a planetary communion. Filter masks worn in the lower industrial tiers acquire a specifically painful valence in this context: to require a mask is to be separated from the breathing world by the occupation's industrial legacy.

The Wheel of Earth movement takes this further. In Wheel theology the Driftweb is a manifestation of the Returning Current in the atmosphere — life completing a cycle from ocean to sky and back to ocean, a visible demonstration that the pattern of return is real and operating at every scale. Some Wheel practitioners maintain small Driftweb cultivation vessels — sealed glass containers with a small amount of the organism growing in a nutrient medium — as meditative objects. The slow growth of the filaments over days and weeks is contemplated as an analogue for the patient work of the Cause.

**Game Notes:** The Driftweb has no direct mechanical interaction with characters at the table. It is texture, atmosphere (literally and figuratively), and world-building infrastructure. Three specific uses:

*Visual:* When the scenario calls for an exterior shot of Gashidda from approach, from orbit, or from the surface, the Driftweb is what the characters see — the grey-green overcast that never fully clears, the slow movement of mat structures visible from the arcology's upper-tier observation decks on still days, the occasional silver veil catching light through a break in the cloud.

*Navigational:* Any scene involving atmospheric flight or vehicle transit should note that the Driftweb layer at 4–8km provides sensor cover. A chase scene, an extraction flight, or an emergency transit could legitimately use the Driftweb layer as cover — at the cost of some navigational precision and at the risk of engine fouling if intake filters are not maintained.

*Plot hook:* Seluun Mian-Ashta's research interests, as noted in the scenario, extend to novel organic chemistry. The Driftweb lipid fraction has properties — specifically its atmospheric stability across the temperature and pressure range of Gashidda's weather systems — that interest Vegan biochemists for reasons that go beyond fuel applications. What exactly those reasons are is left to the referee.

### 01 · GIKHI
*Gashidda Common Reef Eel · Elongichthys gashiddanus*

**Ecological Zone:** Shelf Shallows and Mid-Shelf (0–800m)  
**Size:** 1.2–2.4m length; 8–22kg  
**Behaviour:** Semi-territorial; forms loose feeding aggregations  
**Threat Level:** Negligible (non-aggressive unless cornered)  
**Commercial Value:** ★★★★★ — Primary protein export; eggs are a subsector luxury delicacy

---

**Description:**

The gikhi is Gashidda's most economically significant marine animal and the species most familiar to residents of the undersea arcologies, where gikhi-eel products dominate the food market. It is a large, sinuous creature superficially resembling a terrestrial moray eel, though the two share no evolutionary relationship beyond convergent body plan.

The gikhi's body is muscular and laterally compressed, built for navigating the complex crevice systems of the reef columns. The skin is scaleless — covered instead in a thick, mucous-secreting integument that shifts colour across a narrow range of blue-greens, functioning as both camouflage in the reef and as a chemical communication system. Two rows of small, paddle-like fins run the length of the lower body, providing fine positional control in tight spaces. The head is broad and blunt, with a wide jaw containing multiple rows of small crushing teeth adapted for hard-shelled reef invertebrates.

Gikhi eggs — laid in clutches of 200–400 in deep reef crevices — are approximately 8mm in diameter, semi-translucent amber in colour, with a rich, complex flavour profile that has made them a culinary export throughout the Dingir subsector. The eggs are harvested by licensed diving contractors who locate clutches using acoustic sensors. Wild harvest is strictly managed; the majority of commercial supply comes from arcology-adjacent aquaculture operations.

**Game Notes:** The gikhi is not dangerous under normal circumstances. A cornered or distressed gikhi may bite (1D damage, no armour penetration). A school of gikhi aggregating around a food source is one of the most reliable visual indicators of current patterns in the reef — experienced diving contractors use gikhi behaviour as a navigation aid.

### 02 · REEF LANTERN
*Aggregating Colonial Polyp · Luminocolonia makara*

**Ecological Zone:** Mid-Shelf reef columns (200–2,000m)  
**Size:** Individual polyp 2–4mm; colonies form structures 0.5m–40m tall  
**Behaviour:** Sessile filter feeder; colonial organism  
**Threat Level:** None  
**Commercial Value:** ★★ — Bioluminescent compounds used in medical imaging; reef-column tourism (pre-occupation); arcology aesthetic lighting extracts

---

**Description:**

The reef lantern is not a single animal but a colonial organism — billions of individual polyps building shared calcium-silicate structures that rise from the shelf bedrock in tapering columns, some reaching forty metres in height. These are the "bioluminescent reef columns" visible through Makara's observation bays, and they are what gives Gashidda's deep ocean its characteristic visual identity.

Each polyp generates a cold bioluminescent pulse — the chemistry involves a luciferase-analog enzyme reacting with a copper-containing substrate rather than the iron-based hemoglobin of most Solomani-familiar organisms, which produces the distinctively blue-green colour rather than the yellow-green of most terrestrial bioluminescent life. The pulses are not random: the colony coordinates them through chemical signalling, producing slow wave patterns that travel up and down the column over a period of 8–12 seconds. From a distance, the effect is of light breathing.

Reef lantern colonies are the structural foundation of the entire mid-shelf ecosystem. Their mineral secretions form the substrate on which hundreds of other species live. Their chemical signalling — traces of which diffuse into the surrounding water — is used by many reef-associated species as a navigation and communication reference. The gikhi hunts in reef lantern columns; the Void Mantle (see below) uses them as ambush cover; the Deep Bell (see below) feeds on juvenile polyps.

Imperial development pressures have damaged reef lantern colonies near the New Mondulkiri industrial approaches. Makara's marine scientists consider this the subsector's most significant unreported ecological loss.

**Game Notes:** Reef lantern colonies are the primary navigation reference for unguided diving in the deep shelf. An agent or diver without electronic navigation can use the columns' pulse patterns to maintain orientation: Recon 6+ to read the pattern correctly and maintain directional sense. Failure results in spatial disorientation in the open water between columns.

### 03 · VOID MANTLE
*Pelagic Ambush Predator · Abyssocephalus velatus*

**Ecological Zone:** Mid-Shelf open water adjacent to reef columns (400–1,800m)  
**Size:** Bell diameter 0.8–3.2m; trailing tentacle curtain 4–12m  
**Behaviour:** Solitary ambush predator; nearly invisible at rest  
**Threat Level:** ★★★ — Dangerous to unprotected divers; tentacles cause severe tissue necrosis  
**Commercial Value:** ★ — Tentacle toxin compounds under medical research; not commercially harvested

---

**Description:**

The Void Mantle is the apex ambush predator of the mid-shelf zone, and one of the most visually striking — and dangerous — animals in Gashidda's ocean. It is a large medusan organism: a flattened, near-circular bell of near-perfect optical transparency, trailing a dense curtain of filamentous tentacles below. At rest, floating motionless between reef lantern columns, it is functionally invisible. The bell refracts light rather than absorbing it, and the tentacles are thinner than human hair and colourless. What alerts experienced divers to its presence is the *absence* of small reef organisms in the water immediately around it — the local fauna know it is there.

The bell's upper surface is lined with chemoreceptors of extraordinary sensitivity. When prey passes through the tentacle curtain — which can extend twelve metres below the bell — the tentacles contract around the target in approximately 0.3 seconds. The tentacle toxin is a complex polypeptide neurotoxin that causes immediate intense pain, local tissue necrosis beginning within minutes, and systemic paralysis in larger prey over 10–30 minutes depending on body mass.

The Void Mantle's bell does produce bioluminescence, but only during feeding or apparent stress — a sudden bloom of deep violet-purple light across the bell surface that diving contractors call "the bloom." Seeing the bloom from outside the tentacle radius is a survival warning. Seeing it from inside is typically a sign that the situation has already deteriorated.

**Game Notes:**  
- **Detection:** Recon 10+ (Difficult) to spot a motionless Void Mantle before entering tentacle range. DM+2 if the diver is specifically watching for fauna displacement patterns. DM–2 in low-visibility conditions or if distracted.
- **Tentacle contact:** END 8+ or suffer the following: immediate 2D damage; Medic 8+ within 10 minutes to neutralise spreading tissue necrosis (otherwise 1D damage/hour for 1D6 hours); DEX–2 for 2D hours from early systemic effects.
- **Avoidance:** A motionless diver who has not yet contacted tentacles and who does not exhale bubbles (sealed rebreather required) may avoid triggering the contraction reflex: Athletics (dexterity) 8+ to back out slowly.
- **Standard counter-equipment:** Diving contractors operating in mid-shelf zones carry electrostatic deterrent rods (TL9, Cr 200) that cause the Void Mantle to contract its tentacles away from the stimulus — effectively a repellent. Naval Infantry dive teams are issued these as standard.

### 04 · DEEP BELL
*Schooling Mid-Water Grazer · Bathypelagus campanulus*

**Ecological Zone:** Mid-Shelf and Shelf Shallows (50–1,200m; vertical migration)  
**Size:** Individual 15–35cm bell diameter  
**Behaviour:** Schools of 500–50,000 individuals; daily vertical migration  
**Threat Level:** None  
**Commercial Value:** ★★★ — Significant food source for commercially harvested species; bell-tissue used in filter membrane manufacturing; photogenic (pre-occupation tourism)

---

**Description:**

If the reef lantern colony is the architectural foundation of Gashidda's mid-shelf ecosystem, the Deep Bell is its circulatory system — the organism that moves energy between zones, connects predators to producers, and gives the ocean its characteristic rhythm.

The Deep Bell is a small, elegant medusan organism — much smaller and more delicate than the Void Mantle, and entirely non-predatory. It feeds on the microscopic colonial organisms and chemical compounds produced by the reef lantern polyps, grazing the water column as it migrates vertically with a precision that has fascinated Gashiddan marine biologists. At dusk (or the equivalent timing cycle in the deep ocean, which the Deep Bell tracks through pressure and chemical gradients rather than light), the great schools begin their descent from the shallower feeding zones to the depths of the reef column field, 800–1,200 metres below. At dawn, they rise.

The Deep Bell's bell is semi-opaque, coloured a deep violet-blue with a fringe of short, non-toxic tentacles used only for filter feeding. In large schools, the combined bioluminescent glow of millions of bells produces one of Gashidda's most remarkable natural spectacles — what arcology residents call the "blue drift," a luminous curtain of moving light that passes through the observation bays twice daily. Children press their faces to the transparent hull panels to watch it. Imperial garrison troops have been known to stop to watch it too.

**Game Notes:** A school of Deep Bells in full migration is significant navigation interference for acoustic sensors (DM–2 to Electronics (sensors) checks using sonar during the migration period, twice daily, for approximately 90 minutes each pass). Experienced submersible pilots learn to time their operations around the drift. Attempting to navigate a small submersible through a dense bell school requires Pilot (watercraft) 6+ to avoid disorienting the pilot with the visual effect; failure results in temporary heading error.

The blue drift is also a significant cultural and emotional reference for Gashiddan citizens — particularly for those who support the Vanthara Truth movement, which uses the twice-daily drift as a meditative observance, pausing work and facing the observation bays. Imperial garrison officers who have ordered their soldiers not to interfere with this observance have, on the whole, had better outcomes than those who have not.

### 05 · SHELF ARCHITECT
*Reef-Building Colonial Organism · Constructans gashiddanus*

**Ecological Zone:** Shelf bedrock and column bases (200–600m)  
**Size:** Individual organism microscopic; colony masses 0.1m–several tonnes  
**Behaviour:** Sessile; chemosynthetic and filter-feeding  
**Threat Level:** None directly — colony edge structures are razor-sharp  
**Commercial Value:** ★★★★ — Shell-mineral compounds used in high-tensile construction materials; primary structural substrate of the undersea arcologies

---

**Description:**

The Shelf Architect is the least visually spectacular organism in this bestiary and arguably the most important one on Gashidda. It is the reason the undersea arcologies can exist where they do.

The Shelf Architect is a microscopic colonial organism that secretes a calcium-silicate-iron composite mineral — Gashiddan engineers have named it **arcite** — that is, weight for weight, stronger than most TL9 structural alloys and resistant to the sustained pressure and slight chemical acidity of Gashidda's deep ocean water. Over millions of years, Shelf Architect colonies have built the equatorial reef shelf itself — the vast continuous formation of bioengineered rock on which Makara, and the hundreds of other undersea cities, are constructed. The arcologies are, in a meaningful sense, built on a living foundation.

Individual Shelf Architect organisms are invisible to the naked eye. The colonies they build are visually distinctive: dense, irregular masses of grey-white to pale ochre mineral with a surface texture somewhere between fine coral and rough ceramic. The growing edge of a colony is blade-sharp — the mineral is deposited in thin, overlapping plates — and has caused significant injuries to unprotected divers who contact it.

Active colony growth is visually identifiable by a faint orange-brown biofilm on the leading edge — the living organisms themselves. In areas of healthy reef, the Shelf Architect underlies everything. In areas of industrial damage or chemical contamination — such as the zones near the New Mondulkiri port approaches — the biofilm is absent, and the dead white mineral underneath is visually distinct from the living colony.

**Game Notes:** Arcite mineral fragments make effective improvised cutting tools (1D damage, but they shatter after one use on a failed attack roll). A character who falls or is pushed against a growing colony edge takes 1D damage and must make END 6+ to avoid lacerations that count as open wounds in a contaminated ocean environment (Medic 6+ to prevent infection risk). Reef architects are also used as a dating mechanism — growth-ring analysis of arcite columns allows geologists and archaeologists to date subsurface structures with precision.

### 06 · THERMAL ANGEL
*Deep Vent Chemosynthetic Swimmer · Thermovola gashiddanus*

**Ecological Zone:** Deep Vent Fields (1,500–4,000m)  
**Size:** 0.6–1.1m wingspan equivalent; body mass 0.4–1.2kg  
**Behaviour:** Solitary; closely associated with geothermal vent systems  
**Threat Level:** ★ — Defensive electric discharge if handled; not aggressive  
**Commercial Value:** ★★ — Bioelectric compounds under research; vent-zone survey indicator organism; extreme rarity makes harvest uneconomic and legally restricted

---

**Description:**

The Thermal Angel is the least-known organism in this bestiary — the deep vent fields where it lives have been surveyed by only a handful of unmanned probe missions and two crewed descents, both pre-occupation. What is known comes primarily from remote footage, recovered specimens (three intact, seven partial), and the reports of the Makara Marine Science Institute's vent-survey programme, currently suspended pending Imperial budget review.

The Thermal Angel is a flattened, bilaterally symmetrical organism with two broad, semi-rigid lateral fins extending from a compressed central body — the "wings" that give it its common name, though it moves through the water by undulation rather than flapping. The body is a deep, near-black red-brown in the visible spectrum, absorbing the limited thermal radiation near vent outlets. The surface is covered in a dense mat of modified cilia that absorb dissolved hydrogen sulfide from the vent water — its primary energy source — and the underside of the lateral fins is lined with what appear to be modified electroreceptors that may also function as electrogenerating organs, though the purpose is debated.

The most striking feature of the Thermal Angel in footage is its movement: it hovers nearly motionless in the rising thermal plume above a vent outlet, making micro-adjustments with its fins, for hours at a time. When disturbed, it contracts its fins tightly against its body and drops rapidly into the vent column itself — disappearing into temperatures that would be lethal to most macroscopic organisms. Whether it can survive in the vent outlet itself, or whether this is a predator-escape behaviour that relies on the predator being unwilling to follow, is unknown.

The electric discharge it produces when physically handled is approximately 40–60 volts at low amperage — painful and disorienting rather than dangerous to a healthy adult. Its purpose is unknown. It may be a communication mechanism. It may be a hunting tool not yet observed in the field. It may be a coincidental byproduct of the electroreceptor organ function.

**Game Notes:** Thermal Angels are survey indicator organisms — their presence near a vent system confirms the vent is thermally active and chemically productive, which is relevant information for geothermal infrastructure planning. The deep vent fields are the frontier of Gashidda's known ecology; a scenario or campaign involving classified vent-field survey data would likely have Thermal Angels as a key discovery element.
- **Electric discharge (if handled):** END 8+ or suffer 1D stun damage and DM–1 to all actions for 1D×10 minutes.
- **Survey value:** An intact, documented Thermal Angel sighting (imagery or captured specimen) is worth Cr 8,000–15,000 to the Makara Marine Science Institute or Cr 25,000+ to a private xenobiological research broker.

## NOTES FOR REFEREES

**Using the bestiary in play:**

The deep-sea fauna of Gashidda should function primarily as **atmosphere and world texture** rather than combat encounters. The ocean is alive, and making that vivid — a gikhi darting away from a submersible's lights, the blue drift passing through Makara's observation bays during a tense briefing scene, the warning absence of Deep Bells that signals a Void Mantle nearby — makes the world feel real in a way that benefits every kind of scene.

**The blue drift as cultural touchstone:** The twice-daily Deep Bell migration is explicitly used by the Vanthara Truth movement as a meditative observance. In *Operation Deep Current*, this is a moment where the agents can either respect local custom (building goodwill) or ignore it (losing it). More broadly, the drift is a moment of beauty in a world under occupation — something the citizens of Makara have that cannot be taken from them, a reminder of what Gashidda is beneath its military situation.

**The Void Mantle as environmental threat:** In any scenario involving external diving, submersible hull-walks, or lower-tier arcology flooding events, the Void Mantle can appear as a meaningful hazard without being a cheap monster encounter. Its near-invisibility and its beauty (the violet bloom is genuinely spectacular) make it a predator that feels like part of a real ecosystem rather than an obstacle placed by a game designer.

**The Thermal Angel as plot hook:** The deep vent fields are terra incognita. The Makara Marine Science Institute's vent-survey programme has been suspended by Imperial budget review — but the data it collected before suspension is presumably still in the Institute's archive. What was found down there? What does it mean that the Imperial administration chose to defund the study of it? The Thermal Angel is a mystery with potential answers at 3,000 metres.

**The Driftweb as atmosphere and opportunity:** The Driftweb needs no mechanical rules at the table — it is the world's colour and texture. Every exterior scene on Gashidda happens under its grey-green overcast. Every Vanthara Truth reference to the Second Breath theology gains weight if players understand that the atmosphere they are breathing is biologically active. The scoop-jet harvesting angle is best introduced as background detail — an overheard conversation in the mid-tier market about Seaharvester's new atmospheric division, a parked harvest skimmer at the New Mondulkiri landing field with wide-mouth intakes still trailing dried filaments — and allowed to become a plot element only if the players pursue it. The sensor-cover properties of the 4–8km band are useful for any scenario that needs an extraction route or a chase that the Imperial Army cannot easily monitor.
